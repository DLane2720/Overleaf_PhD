\chapter{Introduction} \label{introduction}
% \subfile{sections/introduction_02}% Opening Context
The rise of autonomous systems is transforming numerous industries, and the maritime sector is no exception. \Acp{USV} have the potential to improve safety and operational efficiency by performing the dull, dirty, and dangerous tasks traditionally done by humans.
Their performance depends on environmental situational awareness, which refers to the system’s ability to perceive relevant features of the surrounding environment. 
Real-time object detection and classification provide the perceptual basis for this capability and are essential for safe navigation and decision making in complex maritime environments.

% Problem Background
Although the automotive industry has achieved significant progress in autonomous perception, comparable advances for maritime platforms remain limited. 
A review of recent literature reveals that studies on autonomous ground vehicles outnumber maritime autonomy publications by nearly one order of magnitude, with a similar disparity observed in object detection research. \textcolor{red}{(cite)}
While some perception strategies from automotive systems can be adapted, the maritime domain introduces fundamentally different challenges. 
Marine environments are unstructured and dynamic, with object densities ranging from sparse to highly dense, particularly in littoral and harbor zones where \acp{USV} frequently operate.

Autonomous maritime systems require high-fidelity sensing to perceive and interact with their immediate operational environment.
Objects must be accurately located and identified so that the \ac{USV} can make informed operational decisions and ensure safe navigation.
Traditional marine radar lacks the spatial resolution needed at these short ranges; instead, uncrewed perception depends primarily on optical and \ac{LiDAR} sensors. \textcolor{red}{(cite)}
Both modalities, however, face significant challenges in dynamic marine conditions.
Visual-spectrum cameras capture rich color and texture information, which is essential for recognizing navigational aids and classifying many objects; however, their performance degrades under conditions such as specular reflections, atmospheric haze, fog, or variable illumination.
Furthermore, camera imagery inherently lacks spatial depth unless supported by additional sensing or estimation methods.
Conversely, \ac{LiDAR} provides precise three-dimensional measurements but lacks the spectral detail required to interpret color-coded or visually distinctive targets.

Real-time fusion is particularly crucial for embedded maritime platforms, where operational demands are high and computational resources are limited.

\section{Modern Vision-Based Object Detection for Autonomy}

\subsection{Deep Learning Foundations and Single-Stage Detectors}

The application of convolutional neural networks to visual recognition gained widespread adoption following Krizhevsky et al.'s demonstration that deep architectures trained on large-scale datasets could achieve superior performance on ImageNet~\cite{krizhevsky2017}.
Subsequent architectural innovations, including residual learning frameworks~\cite{he2016}, demonstrated that network depth correlates with representational capacity for complex visual recognition tasks.
Early object detection methods adapted these classification networks into two-stage pipelines.
Girshick et al.~\cite{girshick2014} introduced \ac{R-CNN}, which applies to region proposals; however, the computational overhead limited throughput.
%%%%% MRC: What limitations?
Ren et al.~\cite{ren2016} addressed these \textcolor{red}{limitations} with Faster \ac{R-CNN}, introducing region proposal networks that enabled end-to-end training and substantially improved inference speed.

Single-stage detectors emerged to address latency constraints by reformulating detection as direct regression from image features to object classes and bounding box coordinates.
The YOLO family exemplifies this paradigm, enabling real-time operation through unified spatial prediction~\cite{redmon2016}.
Subsequent YOLO iterations introduced architectural refinements, including multi-scale detection heads and anchor-free prediction mechanisms \cite{terven2023}.

YOLOv8, released in 2023 as a commercial implementation by Ultralytics \cite{ultralytics}, introduced architectural innovations addressing \textcolor{red}{limitations} of earlier single-stage detectors.
%%%%% MRC: What limitations?
The anchor-free detection strategy employs dual detection heads that independently identify object presence and predict bounding box coordinates, simplifying transfer learning and enhancing adaptability to varying aspect ratios.
% The backbone incorporates Cross-Stage Partial Fusion (C2f) modules that enhance feature reuse while reducing computational overhead compared to earlier designs.
Modern loss functions, including Complete Intersection-over-Union (CIoU) and Distribution Focal Loss, improve localization precision and boundary regression stability~\cite{redmon2016}.
Max pooling and image down-sampling also contribute to CNN speed, but 
impose a fundamental constraint on small object detection (SOD): objects smaller than approximately 16-32 pixels produce low-confidence or unstable detections~\cite{rekavandi2022}.

For maritime applications, down-sampling imagery to 640×640 pixel inputs corresponds to smaller or distant targets near the horizon occupying less than 0.5-1\% of the image frame.
These architectural properties make YOLOv8 suitable for real-time use; however, the model's constraints on SOD, along with other environmental factors, motivate the investigation of complementary sensing modalities.

Visual detection in maritime environments presents challenges that are fundamentally distinct from those in terrestrial scenarios.
Prasad et al.~\cite{prasad2017} identify background motion from waves and wakes, low contrast objects, and environmental degradation as primary failure modes.
High dynamic range presents persistent challenges for maritime detection.

Shao et al.~\cite{shao2022} addressed this through multi-scale detection strategies and deformable convolution modules.
Maritime detection research remains fragmented, lacking standardized large-scale datasets analogous to KITTI or COCO~\cite{zhang2021, yang2024, su2023}.

\section{LiDAR-Based Object Detection for Autonomous Systems}

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches for Point Cloud Processing}

\ac{LiDAR} sensors provide three-dimensional geometric measurements independent of illumination by measuring the time-of-flight of laser pulses.
% Kunz et al.~\cite{kunz2005} demonstrated that \ac{LiDAR} reflections from small maritime targets produce significantly stronger returns than sea surface returns, with small buoys detectable at ranges exceeding 9 kilometers under adverse weather.
Processing point cloud data presents unique computational challenges: unlike images with a regular grid structure, point clouds present non-uniform three-dimensional sampling of the surrounding area~\cite {zhou2018, shi2019}.
This sparse sampling presents opportunities for a variety of methods of detection.

Deep learning has emerged as the dominant paradigm for point cloud object detection, with neural networks designed to learn hierarchical feature representations from three-dimensional data.
These learned approaches leverage large labeled datasets to extract spatial patterns and semantic information directly from point coordinates.
The following architectural families represent the evolution of neural network-based methods, progressing from point-based processing to voxelization strategies and hybrid architectures.

Point-based networks (PointNet~\cite{qi2017}) process raw coordinates directly; however, they scale unfavorably with point cloud size~\cite{shi2019}.
Voxelization methods address scalability by discretizing point clouds into regular grids.
Zhou and Tuzel~\cite{zhou2018} developed VoxelNet, which transforms sparse point clouds into dense volumetric representations.
Yan et al.~\cite{yan2018} extended this with SECOND, introducing sparse convolutional operations that improve efficiency by processing only occupied voxels.
Lang et al.~\cite{lang2019} proposed PointPillars, which encodes point clouds into vertical columns, enabling efficient 2D convolutional processing.
The fundamental trade-off in voxelization is quantization error: small objects or fine geometric details may be lost during the discretization process~\cite {zhou2018}.
Hybrid architectures like point-voxel R-CNN (PV-RCNN)~\cite{shi2019, shi2020} combine the strengths of point-based and voxel-based processing, achieving state-of-the-art automotive accuracy but assuming dense ground plane returns that do not hold for maritime applications where water surfaces produce sparse returns~\cite{kunz2005}.

Recent innovations demonstrate continued advancement, but with substantial computational requirements.
Combining features observed in a shared reference frame with a \ac{BEV}, BEVFusion~\cite{liang2022, liu2023b} achieves state-of-the-art accuracy, but requires desktop-class \ac{GPU}s with substantial power consumption and achieves only 15-20 frames per second~\cite{liu2023b}. 
This power budget exceeds available power for many small autonomous vessels.
Transformer-based architectures that apply self-attention to point clouds offer theoretical advantages but require multi-\ac{GPU} configurations with substantial power consumption and have not been validated under maritime-specific conditions~\cite{xie2024}.

Beyond computational constraints, these neural network-based approaches face limitations for safety-critical maritime applications.
The non-deterministic nature of their architecture presents challenges when enforcing strict rules, such as ensuring object separation distances.
This limitation is particularly problematic when using spatial data for navigational purposes and obstacle avoidance~\cite{coyleE}.
Given this persistent challenge, there is a continued need to investigate deterministic approaches that provide stricter guarantees for safety-critical applications. 

\subsection{Deterministic Geometric Alternatives}

In contrast to neural network-based approaches, deterministic geometric algorithms offer advantages for resource-constrained embedded platforms, where runtime predictability and minimal training data requirements are highly valued.
Coyle~\cite{coyleE} developed Grid-Based Clustering and Concave Hull Extraction (GB-CACHE), a deterministic approach specifically designed for uncrewed systems perceiving objects on flat surfaces, including water.
GB-CACHE efficiently segments point clouds through grid-based spatial partitioning, then extracts concave hull boundaries that accurately represent objects with irregular geometries.

The deterministic nature of GB-CACHE provides specific operational advantages.
Processing latency remains bounded and predictable, eliminating variable inference times characteristic of neural networks, where batch size and scene complexity affect throughput.
Rule enforcement for spatial constraints—critical for obstacle avoidance and navigation—can be rigorously guaranteed, whereas neural approaches provide probabilistic outputs without strict guarantees~\cite{coyleE}.

\subsection{Maritime-Specific LiDAR Challenges}

Maritime \ac{LiDAR} performance differs fundamentally from automotive scenarios.
Automotive systems achieve near 100\% return rates on asphalt, providing dense point clouds with abundant ground plane context~\cite{roriz2022}.
Water surfaces produce specular reflection, yielding only 10\% return rates versus near-100\% on asphalt~\cite{kunz2005, roriz2022}, creating sparse point clouds lacking dense ground plane context exploited by automotive algorithms~\cite{zhou2018, shi2019}.
Kunz et al.~\cite{kunz2005} measured return rates over calm water as low as 10\% of the emitted pulses, noting that water surface characteristics produce significantly weaker returns than small floating targets, creating a favorable contrast for target detection despite a sparse environmental context.

The sparsity of water returns poses challenges to detection algorithms designed for dense point clouds in automotive applications.
Maritime applications lack dense reference, necessitating alternative spatial reasoning strategies when objects appear isolated without contextual grounding.
Atmospheric effects reduce effective range by 50-70\% in heavy haze~\cite{roriz2022}, while wave-induced motion requires IMU-based motion compensation, introducing processing latency~\cite{xie2024, ahmed2024}.
Platform motion caused by continuous wave-induced pitch, roll, and heave motions vary dynamically with sea state.
Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2024} developed specialized Kalman filtering approaches tailored for maritime platform motion, reporting 25-30\% improvement in detection accuracy when compensating for tilt-induced point cloud distortions.

Recent maritime \ac{LiDAR} detection work demonstrates growing interest yet reveals substantial validation gaps.
Xie et al.~\cite{xie2024} achieved an overall detection accuracy of 74.1\% by integrating state-of-the-art networks for ship detection, although performance degraded for vessels under 5 meters and at ranges exceeding 40 meters.
Detection methods designed for dense ground plane returns require fundamental adaptation for sparse maritime point clouds where objects lack grounding context~\cite{kunz2005}.
A systematic evaluation of detection performance versus water surface characteristics, motion compensation quality, and embedded platform profiling (including latency, power, and throughput) is absent from the reviewed literature~\cite{xie2024, ahmed2024}.

The complementary limitations of camera and \ac{LiDAR} modalities—vision failing under extreme lighting while LiDAR struggles with sparse returns—suggest potential benefits from sensor fusion.

\section{Sensor Fusion Paradigms}

Sensor fusion combines information from multiple modalities to overcome the limitations of individual sensors.
Recent surveys~\cite{feng2021, tufekci2023, huang2024a} identify fusion level—early, mid, or late—as the primary architectural distinction determining computational requirements, calibration sensitivity, and failure mode propagation relevant to maritime deployment.
Each fusion paradigm offers distinct trade-offs between peak accuracy potential, robustness to sensor degradation, and real-time implementation feasibility~\cite{feng2021}.

\subsection{Early Fusion: Data-Level Integration}

Early fusion combines raw sensor data before feature extraction, projecting measurements from different modalities into a common representation~\cite{cui2022}.
Cui et al.~\cite{cui2022} note that early fusion enables networks to learn joint features during training.
PointPainting exemplifies this paradigm by projecting \ac{LiDAR} points into camera image coordinates, assigning semantic labels from segmentation networks, then processing the semantically enriched point cloud through object detectors~\cite{cui2022}.

%%%%%%%%% clean up the AI produced colons and hyphens %%%%%%%%%%%
However, early fusion imposes stringent calibration and synchronization requirements, creating practical deployment challenges.
Projection errors of even a few degrees of rotation or centimeters of translation misalign features, corrupting the joint representation~\cite{cui2022}.
Temporal synchronization must also be precise; misaligned timestamps create spatial inconsistencies, degrading fusion quality.
Sensor-specific noise and failures propagate directly: corrupted camera images from lens occlusion or extreme lighting produce degraded semantic labels affecting all projected \ac{LiDAR} points~\cite{cui2022}.
For maritime platforms experiencing continuous wave-induced motion and intermittent sun glare, calibration sensitivity and failure propagation of early fusion represent significant operational risks.
Maritime platform dynamics introduce additional calibration complexity beyond automotive scenarios.
While automotive research established general calibration tolerances~\cite{cui2022}, wave-induced motion can create time-varying calibration errors even when initial alignment is precise.
%%%%%%% MRG: why only comparing to ground vehicles
These dynamic calibration challenges affect all fusion paradigms but with varying severity depending on the required precision of spatial correspondence.

\subsection{Mid Fusion: Feature-Level Integration}

Mid fusion extracts features independently from each modality before combining them in intermediate network layers~\cite{huang2024a}.
Huang et al.~\cite{huang2024a} systematically compare fusion paradigms, finding that mid fusion balances accuracy and robustness. 
Independent feature extraction prevents errors in individual sensor data from propagating between modalities, yet learned fusion layers capture cross-modal correlations unavailable to late fusion.

BEVFusion~\cite{liang2022, liu2023b} transforms camera features into \ac{BEV} representations compatible with \ac{LiDAR} BEV features, combining them through efficient convolution operations.
Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023b} note that BEVFusion achieves state-of-the-art performance but requires desktop-class \ac{GPU} hardware, exceeding embedded platform capabilities by an order of magnitude.
For battery-powered maritime platforms with constrained power budgets, mid-fusion's computational demands preclude direct deployment without substantial architectural optimization.

\subsection{Late Fusion: Decision-Level Integration}

Late fusion operates on independent object detections from each modality, combining outputs through spatial association~\cite{wang2020a, pang2020}.
This approach maintains modular detection pipelines, fusing results at the decision level after individual sensor processing is complete.
While sacrificing joint feature learning enabled by early or mid-fusion, late fusion offers architectural properties advantageous for resource-constrained maritime deployments.

Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020a} survey multi-sensor fusion in automated driving, emphasizing that late fusion enables graceful degradation: sensor failures affect only individual detection streams without corrupting fusion outputs.

Pang et al.~\cite{pang2020} developed \acp{CLOC}, which fuse detection candidates before non-maximum suppression and train networks to predict the fused detection quality based on geometric and semantic consistency across modalities.

Late fusion's modular architecture provides several practical advantages for maritime deployment.
Computational requirements scale approximately linearly with sensor count rather than exponentially as with joint processing~\cite{wang2020a}.
Processing latency remains predictable because each modality follows fixed pipelines without runtime cross-modal dependencies during feature extraction.
Adding or removing sensors requires minimal system redesign—new detection streams integrate through updated fusion modules without modifying existing pipelines~\cite{feng2021}.
Most critically for maritime applications, individual sensor failures degrade performance gracefully rather than causing catastrophic fusion failure~\cite{huang2024a}.
Temporal synchronization requirements are relaxed compared to early fusion: late fusion spatial association operates on object-level regions rather than pixel-level correspondences, tolerating larger timing offsets—typically 50 to 100 milliseconds versus 10 milliseconds for early fusion~\cite{huang2024a}.
For maritime platforms where precise hardware synchronization may be impractical and wave-induced motion creates apparent object movement, relaxed timing tolerances represent operational advantages.

\section{Research Gaps and Dissertation Motivation}

Despite substantial progress in multimodal perception for autonomous systems, several critical gaps remain for maritime applications.
These gaps span algorithmic approaches, computational implementation, and empirical validation resources.

Most published fusion research emphasizes early or mid-level integration, with limited evaluation of late-level approaches under maritime conditions.
Comparative studies in automotive contexts demonstrate that late fusion offers superior robustness under sensor dropout or environmental degradation—conditions frequently encountered during maritime operations~\cite{huang2024a, wang2020a}.
However, these findings primarily derive from terrestrial datasets where environmental characteristics and sensor failure modes differ substantially from those in maritime scenarios.
Whether late fusion maintains similar robustness advantages under maritime-specific challenges, including water reflections, HDR lighting extremes, and platform motion, remains an open empirical question in the reviewed literature.
This dissertation addresses this gap through systematic evaluation of late fusion performance under maritime conditions, as described in Chapter~\ref{realtime_object_detection}.

Real-time fusion implementation on embedded maritime hardware remains underexplored.
Early and mid-fusion methods often exceed the computational budgets of embedded platforms, requiring desktop-class \ac{GPU}s with typical power consumption of 200-400 watts for real-time operation—specifications incompatible with small, battery-powered autonomous vessels.
Late fusion's reduced computational requirements suggest potential feasibility for embedded deployment; however, systematic evaluation of accuracy-latency-power trade-offs on desktop-class maritime hardware is lacking in prior maritime fusion research.
This dissertation evaluates YOLO model variants %(nano, small, medium) 
on Minion's hardware (NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 \ac{GPU}), establishing baseline performance and selecting the small variant for its equivalent accuracy to medium with improved inference speed, as detailed in Chapter~\ref{realtime_object_detection}.
Power consumption is particularly critical for battery-operated platforms where computing loads directly affect mission endurance.

Few labeled multimodal maritime datasets exist with synchronized \ac{LiDAR} and camera observations under diverse conditions \textcolor{red}{(cite)}.
Existing datasets are either single-modality\textcolor{red}{(cite)}, which limits fusion research, lack precise synchronization\textcolor{red}{(cite)}, preventing temporal analysis, or provide limited weather and lighting diversity\textcolor{red}{(cite)}, thereby restricting generalization assessment.
This data gap directly impedes algorithm development and validation.
For this research, a synchronized \ac{LiDAR}-camera dataset was collected aboard the Minion platform during operational deployments, providing 778 annotated images with 1934 labeled maritime objects. Details pertaining to this dataset are described in greater detail in Section~\ref{sec:sensor_data_dataset}.

This dissertation develops and validates a late-fusion framework for real-time \ac{LiDAR}-camera perception on embedded maritime platforms.
The approach is motivated by the advantages of late fusion for maritime deployment, including a modular architecture that isolates sensor failures, computational efficiency compatible with embedded hardware constraints, and robustness to calibration and synchronization.
Research methodology quantitatively evaluates accuracy, timing, and computational efficiency through controlled experiments and real-world validation conducted aboard the WAMV research platform.

\subsubsection{Image Resolution}
\label{image_resolution}
For a fixed FOV, camera resolution determines which targets can be resolved at a given sensing distance. 
Ultimately, adequate perception across the operational envelop requires resolving the smallest object at the maximum required detection range. 
The relationship between an object's physical width $\mathit{w}_{obj}$ and pixel width within an image $\mathit{w}_{\text{px}}$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pixel_width}
\mathit{w}_{\text{px}} = f \; \frac{N_x}{S_w}\frac{\mathit{w}_{obj}}{d}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is the \ac{EFL} of the camera and lens, $N_x$ is the horizontal image resolution in pixels, and $S_w$ is the width of the image sensor.
This equation can be used to determine camera requirements if the true size of the objects is known, or to determine a reasonable expectation of object resolution if the camera values are known.
This relationship is also critical for determining the distortion present in each camera/lens system, detailed further in section~\ref{spatial_calibration}.

Defining the minimum resolution requirement for an object in the image frame requires an understanding of the object detection method used with visual cameras (discussed in Section~\ref{yolo}), as well as the objects being detected (discussed in Section~\ref{sec:sensor_data_dataset}). 
This image detection architecture is discussed in section ~\ref{yolo}.
Here, it is sufficient to know that this method scales each input image to $640 \times 640$ pixels for processing efficiency.
This should be considered when defining the minimum pixel density of a camera's sensor, as it alters (\ref{eq:pixel_width}), shrinking the pixel width of the object from  $\mathit{w}_{\text{px}}$ to $\mathit{w}'_{\text{px}}$ when processed. $\mathit{w}'_{\text{px}}$ is then defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pixel_width2}
\mathit{w}'_{\text{px}} = f \; \frac{640 \text{px}}{S_w}\frac{\mathit{w}_{obj}}{d}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Dynamic Range}
\label{dynamic_range}

Dynamic range describes the ratio between the brightest and darkest signal levels a camera sensor can record.
Light levels that exceed the camera sensor's range cause the image to appear white or ``blown-out'', while light levels that are too low will appear darker, with detail being indistinguishable from noise.
\acp{USV} and \acp{AGS} routinely operate in environments where bright sky reflections and deep shadows are common, often within the same scene.
Therefore, selecting a camera with insufficient dynamic range may lead to washed-out signals in direct sunlight or lost detail in shadows.

Dynamic range, expressed in dB, is given by
\begin{equation}
 \text{Dynamic Range (dB)} = 20 \log{\left( \frac{N_{sat}}{N_{noise}}\right) }
\end{equation}
where $N_{sat}/N_{noise}$ is the ratio of the saturation to the minimum signal a camera sensor can detect above background noise.
\ac{HDR} imaging can be achieved in two primary ways. 
The first is sequential multi-exposure \ac{HDR}, in which multiple frames captured at different exposure settings are combined to extend the tonal range \cite{Reinhard2010}. An example is provided in Figure \ref{fig:hdr_example}.
While effective for static scenes, as the relative velocity between the sensor and objects within the frame increases, ghosting and motion blur can occur.
The second method uses an in-sensor technique called dual-conversion gain (DCG) that combines multiple light exposure levels within a single frame of exposure.

The reduction in frame rate is essential to maintain reliable network performance given limited bandwidth, as discussed in Section \ref{comp:network}. 
The maximum bitrate $R_{\text{max}}$ of each stream is computed at runtime as a function of the target frame rate, frame resolution, and compression ratio, defined by

\begin{equation}
    R_{\text{max}} = N_x N_y b f
\end{equation}
where $N_x$ and $N_y$ are the image width and height in pixels, $f$ is the desired frame rate, and $b$ is the effective bits per pixel after compression.
For the HDR camera, the image resolution is $2880 \times 1860$ pixels, encoded in YUV 4:2:0 format using H.264 compression.
An empirically derived constant of $b = 0.14$ bits per pixel captures the combined effects of color subsampling and compression efficiency under typical scene conditions.
These values yield a bitrate for the HDR camera of
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        R_{\text{max}} & = (2880 \times 1860\; \text{px}) (0.14 \;\text{bits/px})(5\;\text{s}^{-1})  \\
        & \approx 3.75\; \text{Mbps}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
and scales linearly with frame rate.
This adaptive bitrate approach ensures that each video stream remains within the available network bandwidth while maintaining adequate visual fidelity for perception and data logging.
By default, each camera operates at 5 \ac{fps}, aligning with the data rate of other perception sensors, but this rate can be adjusted in the future using this approach. % to accommodate specific mission or testing requirements.

While compressed video is streamed to Atlas, the Jetson simultaneously records each feed at its native frame rate to the onboard 500 GB solid-state NVMe drive as redundant backups. 
Each recording is saved in 120-second segments along with a corresponding \ac{CSV} file pairing every image frame with a timestamp from the system clock. 
The same system clock provides the \ac{PTP} signal used by the Livox LiDAR units for time synchronization.
This ensures consistent temporal alignment across all sensors within the perception suite. 
Consequently, the timestamps in the recorded \ac{CSV} files are also essential for validating the synchronization of the camera and LiDAR, as discussed in Section \ref{time_sync_cam}.

\section{Sensor Calibration} \label{sec:calibration}
% \subfile{sections/calibration}

The multi-sensor perception suite described in the previous section integrates complementary sensing modalities: \ac{LiDAR} for spatial awareness and cameras for visual context.
Each of these sensors measures the environment within its own local reference frame and according to its own internal clock.
To combine their observations into the same reference frame, the system must first be calibrated both spatially and temporally.

For the Minion platform, all sensors are rigidly mounted and assigned reference frames within a unified transform hierarchy. 
As these transforms are static, a static transform publisher is used to publish....

The GPS receiver provides the USV pose within the global map frame and serves as the anchor for the remainder of the system. 
Each sensor frame is related through a sequence of rigid transforms that define the fixed spatial relationship between components. 
These transforms allow any point, vector, or detection to be expressed in any other frame by applying the appropriate chain of translations and rotations.
Spatial calibration is required to estimate the rigid transforms that populate this hierarchy. 
Intrinsic calibration is also required for visible-spectrum cameras to determine internal optical properties such as focal length, principal point location, and lens distortion. 
Time synchronization is similarly structured (and discussed in greater detail in Section~\ref{time_sync}),
with a master clock distributed over the network to subsystems and sensors to ensure that data acquired across sensor modalities remains temporally aligned.


Within this framework, the port and starboard Livox units are first registered to the center Livox reference frame to produce a unified point cloud. 
The consolidated LiDAR frame is then related to the HDR camera frame through an additional extrinsic transform.

% All perception data are synchronized and expressed in the global map frame, defined by the time, location, and orientation provided by the \ac{GPS}.
Using the camera’s intrinsic parameters, three-dimensional LiDAR points $(x, y, z)$ can then be projected onto the two-dimensional image plane and expressed as pixel locations $(u, v)$. 
An additional series of extrinsic transforms relates the central Livox frame to the \ac{GPS} antenna frame, and the \ac{GPS} frame to the global map frame. 
The \ac{GPS} provides dual functionality, maintaining synchronization across all sensor data and orienting them within the static global map frame.

A process for calibrating these sensors was presented previously in the work of Thompson~\cite{thompson2023}. This work also established baseline intrinsic camera parameters and the extrinsic relationship between the three Livox LiDAR units.
However, in moving the enclosure to the Minion USV the orientation of the LiDAR sensors was changed to pitch down more toward the water, the center Livox had to be replaced due to hardware failure, and the relative location of the PinPoint is different than the GPS used in the previous study. 
As a result, the entire system needed to be re-calibrated for using on Minion.
Furthermore, the prior work failed to perform online time synchronization of the video stream from the NVIDIA Jetson to the Atlas computer.
This dissertation will therefore present a new strategy for time synchronization of large video streams from one compute module to another. 

Section \ref{spatial_calibration} provides the methods used for intrinsic camera calibration, extrinsic calibration between the camera and LiDAR sensors, and extrinsich calibration between the individual LiDAR units. 
This is followed by a discussion of the time synchronization methods implemented across the network, with special detail provided to the new timestamping method applied to video frames in Section \ref{time_sync}.

\subsection{Spatial Calibration} \label{spatial_calibration}

Calibration begins with determining the camera’s intrinsic parameters, followed by estimation of the extrinsic transformation that defines the rigid-body relationship between the camera and each \ac{LiDAR} sensor.  
The following sections describe the methods and results of this calibration process.

\subsubsection{Camera Intrinsics} \label{camera_intrinsics}
% \subfile{sections/cam_intrinsics} % Shold be camera_intrinsics

Camera intrinsic parameters describe the internal geometry of the camera and define how three-dimensional points are projected onto the two-dimensional image plane.  
They are governed by the optical pathway through the lens and determine how real-world distances correspond to image pixel coordinates.  
The intrinsic model is typically divided into an idealized pinhole projection and a lens distortion model.

Under the pinhole model, the projection of a point onto the image plane is given by the intrinsic matrix:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{K} = 
    \begin{bmatrix}
        f_x & s & c_x \\
        0 & f_y & c_y \\
        0 & 0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

where $(f_x, f_y)$ are focal lengths in pixels, $(c_x, c_y)$ denotes the principal point, and $s$ represents the skew factor (generally $s=0$ for modern sensors).

Real lenses introduce distortion, primarily radial, due to refraction near the outer edge of the lens.  
This effect, most pronounced in wide-angle optics, causes straight lines to appear curved.  
Radial distortion is modeled using polynomial coefficients $k_1$, $k_2$, and $k_3$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        x' &= x_n(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6), \\
        y' &= y_n(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

where $(x', y')$ are distorted coordinates, $(x_n, y_n)$ are ideal coordinates, and $r = \sqrt{x_n^2 + y_n^2}$ is the radial distance from the optical center.  
Although this model omits asymmetric distortions, high-quality lenses typically minimize such effects.  
Even with manufacturer-provided parameters, recalibration ensures sub-pixel accuracy when aligning \ac{LiDAR} projections with image data.

To estimate intrinsic parameters, images of a planar checkerboard with known square dimensions is captured from multiple orientations and distances.  
Its grid of high-contrast corners serves as stable reference points for gradient-based corner detection algorithms such as the Harris method.  
Calibration software uses these detected points to compute statistically optimal parameters across the full image set.

Effective calibration requires dozens of images taken at diverse depths and orientations spanning the camera’s operational field of view.  
Checkerboard dimensions should approximate the scale of observed objects to ensure that the chosen camera provides adequate spatial resolution (as discussed in Section~\ref{image_resolution}).  
Following these practices produces robust intrinsic estimates across the full imaging range.

\subsubsection{Camera to LiDAR Extrinsic Calibration} \label{camLidar_calib}
% \subfile{sections/camlidar_calib}


% \subsubsection{Camera to LiDAR Extrinsic Calibration} \label{camLidar_calib}

Camera–LiDAR calibration begins by positioning the planar checkerboard target within both the LiDAR and the camera's field of view.  
The target’s corners (not the checkerboard corners) in the LiDAR frame $(X_L, Y_L, Z_L)$ are matched to corresponding pixel coordinates $(u,v)$ in the image, yielding an initial estimate of the rigid transformation between sensor frames.  
A full derivation of this mapping is given in Appendix~\ref{img_tform}, and an example is shown in Figure~\ref{HDR_calib_final}.

Initial calibration is performed with MATLAB's LiDAR Calibration tool \cite{matlab_calibration}.
The same checkerboard images used for intrinsic camera calibration are reused here and combined with LiDAR point cloud data.
This allows the MATLAB tool to co-locate the planar geometry of the calibration target within regions of interest (ROIs) of the point cloud data.
An \ac{ICP} method is again used to minimize the error between the LiDAR and image frames.
% This software application is s
% Further refinement can be achieved either through iterative manual adjustment or by applying an \ac{ICP} algorithm that minimizes the point-to-plane error between the LiDAR point cloud and the camera-projected checkerboard corners.  
This approach produces a rough estimate of the necessary transform. Manual refinement is required to achieve results demonstrated in Figures~\ref{HDR_calib_final} and \ref{HDR_calib_final_zoom}.
This manual fine-tuning process also allows for minor adjustments to the camera intrinsics where necessary.

Manual refinement is performed after the initial calibration to achieve the required accuracy for reliable fusion. 
The estimated extrinsic and intrinsic parameters generated in MATLAB are used to project LiDAR points into the camera image frame, where they are aligned with corresponding image features.
As the depth information is lost in this process, small portions of the point cloud are isolated to better define depth-related features within the image.
Minor adjustments are then applied to the rotation and translation components of the extrinsic transform, as well as to the focal length, optical center, and radial distortion terms of the intrinsic model.
Distant features such as long walls and window lines reveal minor angular errors, while closer objects with sharp edges, such as poles and fences, expose residual translational and distortion-related errors. Combinations of near-, mid-, and far-field objects help fine-tune the focal length and optical center. The refined calibration is then verified by examining the alignment of LiDAR returns on distant features, where even minor errors are magnified, and by confirming that the projection remains accurate across the full depth of the scene.

\subsubsection{LiDAR Extrinsic Calibration} \label{extrinsic_tform}
% \subfile{sections/extrinsic_tform}

Extrinsic calibration defines the rigid-body transformation between two reference frames.  

The transformation from frame $A$ to frame $B$ is represented as
\begin{equation}
    _{A}^{B}\mathbf{T} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        _{A}^{B}\mathbf{R} & _{A}^{B}\mathbf{t} \\
        \mathbf{0}^\mathrm{T} & 1
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation}

where $_{A}^{B}\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is the rotation matrix describing the orientation of frame $A$ relative to frame $B$, and $_{A}^{B}\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the translation vector locating the origin of frame $A$ with respect to frame $B$.  
A point $_{A}\mathbf{P}$ expressed in frame $A$ can therefore be transformed into frame $B$ as

\begin{equation}
    ^{B}\mathbf{P} =
    _{A}^{B}\mathbf{R} _{A}\mathbf{P} + _{A}^{B}\mathbf{t}.
\end{equation}

This formulation provides a consistent framework for representing spatial relationships among all platform sensors.  
Transformations can be sequentially composed through the transform tree shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tf_tree}, allowing points measured by any sensor to be expressed in a common reference frame and projected into the camera image for spatial alignment.
It is also necessary to record the time at which each measurement is collected. 
This ensures that objects with relative motion are correctly placed in their destination frame, since the positions of both the vessel and the surrounding environment may change between sensor observations.

The \ac{LiDAR}-to-\ac{LiDAR} extrinsic calibration defines the rigid-body transformations relating the three Livox Horizon units to a shared coordinate system.  
The center unit serves as the reference frame, with the port and starboard sensors calibrated relative to it to create a unified, geometrically consistent point cloud.

Using large flat surfaces such as walls or other well-defined objects within the unit's overlapping field of view allows the sensors to be aligned.
The Livox devices used have a functional detection range of 260~m \cite{livox_manual}, which is far greater than the intended operational range for the sensor fusion presented in this research. 
Due to the divergent nature of the scanning pattern, a simple visual inspection  (as illustrated in figure \ref{fig:Lidar2Lidar}) of sensor alignment beyond 100~m guarantees alignment at closer range.

\subsection{Temporal Calibration} \label{time_sync}
% \subfile{sections/time_sync}

This section discusses how each of the distributed computers and sensor are kept in sync with each other.
Section \ref{time_sync_lan} discusses how a master clock time from the GPS is distributed throughout the network, and section \ref{time_sync_cam} discusses how camera data is transferred from one machine to another without loss of timing information.
It is important to note that the temporal offsets discussed here should not be affected by the network latency described in Section~\ref{comp:network}, as timing information is transmitted with the data, and not subject to how long it takes to be delivered. 

Multi-modal sensor fusion for object detection fundamentally requires precise temporal alignment between sensors operating on independent clocks and sampling at different rates.  
For example, if two sensors are offset by 200 milliseconds while observing a vessel moving at $5~\mathrm{m/s}$ and located 30~m from the platform, their detections would appear approximately $1~\mathrm{m}$ apart—equivalent to nearly $2^{\circ}$ of angular separation in the field of view.  
Such a discrepancy disrupts the spatial alignment between LiDAR points and image features, emphasizing the importance of consistent timing across all data streams.
While an inter-sensor offset of 20 milliseconds or less is ideal for real-world scenarios involving moving targets, this level of precision was not necessary for the RobotX competition, where all objects of interest were stationary. As a result, the timing requirement was relaxed to 100 milliseconds prior to data collection.

\subsubsection{Camera Synchronization} \label{time_sync_cam}

Accurate video timestamps are essential for aligning image data with LiDAR point clouds. To achieve frame-level temporal precision, the Minion PC embeds timestamps directly into the H.264/H.265 video stream generated by the Jetson Xavier using the GStreamer framework. This ensures that timing information remains synchronized with the frame itself, surviving compression, transmission, and playback.

% \paragraph{prior solution}
Earlier implementations of the Minion perception system—originally developed by Thompson~\cite{thompson2023}—employed a custom GStreamer plugin that performed two time-stamping operations. 
First, it generated an adjacent .csv file containing timestamps aligned to each frame of the locally recorded video. 
Second, it embedded the same timestamps into the closed-caption (CEA-608) layer of the \ac{RTSP} video stream. 
While effective for offline data processing using the saved video files, this method was limited by the asynchronous nature of \ac{RTSP}, which cannot maintain a persistent link between closed-caption data and video frames. 
This limitation motivated the development of the \ac{SEI}-based synchronization method described below.
This implementation replaces the caption-based approach with direct timestamp embedding using \ac{SEI} Network Abstraction Layer (NAL) units defined in the H.264/H.265 standard.
Each video frame receives a GPS-disciplined system timestamp from the NVIDIA Jetson at the instant of capture. These timestamps are stored as 64-bit values (milliseconds since Unix epoch) and attached to each frame via a custom GStreamer extension. % (CustomTimestampMeta).
This method keeps the timestamp data inextricably linked to the same video layer transmitted over \ac{RTSP}, ensuring that each received video frame has an accurate timestamp, even when dropped frames are encountered.
The complete video processing pipeline is visualized as a block diagram in Appendix \ref{append_video_pipeline}.

At the receiving end, the Atlas PC decodes incoming streams using a complementary GStreamer pipeline equipped with a pad probe on the \texttt{h264parse} element. This probe identifies SEI NAL units and extracts the embedded timestamp payload before decoding. The recovered timestamps are then written to the ROS message headers of the corresponding image frames via the \texttt{image\_transport} framework.

\subsubsection{Network Synchronization} \label{time_sync_lan}

The Minion platform employs a hierarchical time-synchronization system that distributes GPS-disciplined time from the PinPoint receiver to all perception sensors and computing nodes. 
The GPS module serves as the master time source, and its clock is propagated across the local network via both \ac{NTP} and \acl{PTP} (PTP) services \cite{ptp2008}. 
% PTP standard covers NTP, PTP, and 1588 protocols
These two mechanisms work together to unify the timestamps across the three separate computing systems and LiDAR sensors.

The synchronization hierarchy cascades timing information from the PinPoint GPS receiver, through the Atlas computing cluster, to the Jetson Xavier in the camera enclosure, and finally to each Livox Horizon LiDAR unit. This hierarchy is intentionally structured to align with the system’s physical and logical topology:

At the top of this chain, the PinPoint GPS module provides Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) with sub-microsecond accuracy via the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS). 
This external clock acts as the authoritative reference for the entire vessel, ensuring that all downstream systems inherit a consistent time base.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% \subsubsection{Network Time Protocol} \label{NTP}
% \paragraph{NTP}
Within Minion's \ac{LAN}, a Chrony \ac{NTP} daemon operates on the primary Atlas computer, serving as the authoritative time server for all onboard devices.
Chrony continuously disciplines the system clock using the GPS reference and corrects for minor drift or oscillatory instability.
The NVIDIA Jetson inside the camera enclosure operates as an \ac{NTP} client, synchronizing its system clock with the Atlas PC.
The resulting clock alignment achieves approximately 1–10 milliseconds of accuracy, which is sufficient for coordinating 10 Hz camera streams and maintaining consistent ROS timestamps across computing nodes.
The synchronization status is verified at startup by onboard scripts that query Chrony before any video or LiDAR processes begin. 
This verification ensures that recording never proceeds when clocks are unsynchronized.
% In addition, automated diagnostic checks allow operators to confirm that time distribution remains stable throughout offshore missions, where the GPS link represents the sole external time authority

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% \subsubsection{Precision Time Protocol} \label{PTP}
% \paragraph{PTP}
While \ac{NTP} provides millisecond-level precision for cameras and computing nodes, LiDAR sensors demand tighter synchronization. Each Livox Horizon operates at 100~Hz with microsecond-timestamped measurements, where even millisecond-level drift would cause measurable spatial distortion during vessel motion. To meet this requirement, the Jetson Xavier distributes the \ac{PTP} signal using the Linux ptp4l service.

In this configuration, the Jetson acts as the \ac{PTP} master clock server, broadcasting timing packets over the camera-enclosure network to all three Livox devices, which function as \ac{PTP} clients. \ac{PTP} uses hardware-timestamped Ethernet packets to measure network delay symmetrically and correct for it in real time, achieving sub-microsecond synchronization between LiDAR units. 
The Livox Viewer utility verifies that synchronization with an “active 1588 signal” message once synchronization is established.

\subsubsection{Temporal Synchronization Results} \label{results_time_sync_cam}

An initial evaluation of the video stream and timestamping system was conducted under conditions simulating expected network load. 
Video data were recorded from the single HDR camera for 14.5 minutes at 10 \ac{fps} (twice the anticipated operational rate), yielding 8,720 frames. 
The NVIDIA Jetson in the camera enclosure was accessed via SSH, and the terminal displayed a log of timestamps embedded in the outgoing RTSP video stream. 
Similarly, the Atlas PC displayed the timestamps decoded from the received RTSP feed. 
These values were also written to text files on both machines for comparison. 
To establish a real-world temporal reference, a real-time digital clock was positioned within the camera’s field of view and served as the subject of the video feed, allowing the transmitted timestamp to be visually compared with the actual wall-clock time. 
An example of this setup is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:time_sync1}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Images_old/time_sync1.png}
\caption{Verification of the custom GStreamer pipeline that embeds timestamp data within the RTSP stream. The figure shows the video feed of a real-time clock, transmitted from the camera enclosure to Atlas, used to monitor temporal drift. Terminal windows in the background display the encoded and decoded timestamps, validating pipeline integrity.}
\label{fig:time_sync1}
\end{figure}

The results of this controlled test showed a consistent transport delay of approximately 127 ms and demonstrated that no frames were dropped; critically, the sent and received timestamps matched exactly for all 8,720 frames.

During this test, system-wide clock synchronization was monitored using the NTP software Chrony, which reported an average offset of $197 \pm 33 \text{ms}$ between the NVIDIA Jetson and Atlas machines. 
Although this value appears large at first glance, it reflects Chrony’s behavior during its initial convergence rather than its steady-state accuracy. 
When the time difference between two clocks exceeds 200 ms, Chrony applies coarse corrections followed by gradual, high-precision adjustments to bring the clocks into alignment \cite{chrony}. 
Despite these favorable test results, a subsequent analysis of the ROS bagfiles revealed measurable temporal drift between the original frame timestamps recorded on the Jetson and those captured by the Atlas system, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:temporal drift}. 
Each image received by Atlas was sorted by timestamp and compared with the corresponding image frame saved locally to the NVIDIA Jetson.
The discontinuities seen in the upper plot are caused by gaps in data recording due to overnight system reboots or extended idle periods.
The lower plot examines the rate of this drift and reveals a strikingly consistent accumulation of drift at 0.16 $\mu s$ per frame, or 1.6 $\mu s$ per second.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Images_old/temporal_drift.png}
    \caption{Temporal drift between received image frame timestamp, and timestamp data saved with local video file on the NVIDIA Jetson. The top plot shows the difference in timestamps for each indexed image saved within ROS bags. The bottom plot shows that the drift rate is consistent and linear.}
    \label{fig:temporal drift}
\end{figure}

The regularity of the drift indicates that it is not caused by Chrony’s NTP convergence behavior or thermal instability of the Jetson clock. Instead, the effect likely arises from the coexistence of two separate timestamping methods within the camera streaming code.
The original timestamp method is applied only to the branch of the video signal saved for local backup. In contrast, the new timestamp-embedding method is applied to the branch streamed over the network.
Because these two methods were not unified in the software at the time of data collection, their offset accumulated predictably over the duration of each recording session.

To correct this drift, structural similarity (SSIM) scores were used to match each ROS-recorded frame in the Atlas to its corresponding source frame from the original Jetson video files as a data preprocessing step. 
SSIM is conventionally used to quantify degradation in streamed video quality, but was repurposed as a frame-matching metric.
Correctly paired frames yielded SSIM scores near 1.0, whereas mismatches yielded scores near 0.0. The SSIM metric is defined as
\begin{equation}
\text{SSIM}(x, y) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + C_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + C_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + C_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + C_2)},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_x$ and $\mu_y$ denote mean pixel intensities, $\sigma_x^2$ and $\sigma_y^2$ the variances, $\sigma_{xy}$ the covariance, and $C_1$, $C_2$ small stabilizing constants.
Image pairs with high similarity yield SSIM values near 1.0, while mismatches yield much lower values.

Although the drift rate between the Jetson and Atlas clocks was highly consistent, the initial temporal offset for each ROS bag was unknown. To determine this offset, the first five ROS image messages in each bag were manually paired with their corresponding source frames in the backup video, which are recorded at the native 25 fps (five times the rate of the ROS stream). 
The average offset determined from these manually paired frames established the time difference between the ROS timestamps and the video timeline for each bag.
This initial offset was then applied to all remaining ROS image messages within that bag file, allowing a predicted frame number and timestamp to be generated for each message.
Because the backup video contains five times as many frames as the downsampled ROS stream, a small search window of candidate video frames was examined around each predicted timestamp. 
The SSIM score was calculated for each frame in this window against the video frame in the ROS message.
In this context, SSIM served as a reliable frame-matching score, with correctly aligned frames producing values between 0.875 and 0.9. Images that were off by a single frame had SSIM values that were consistently 0.2 smaller.

The primary temporal contribution of this work was the development of a GStreamer-based video pipeline that embeds GPS-disciplined system timestamps directly into the H.264/H.265 stream using \ac{SEI} \ac{NAL} units.

To recover accurate alignment, SSIM scores were used as a post-processing metric for frame matching.
SSIM provided a reliable means of pairing each ROS image with its corresponding frame from the source video, enabling the removal of temporal drift and the reconstruction of a unified timeline across all camera data.

Real-time object detection lies at the core of autonomous perception, enabling vehicles to interpret their surroundings and respond safely to dynamic environments.
Effective detection requires identifying, classifying, and localizing objects rapidly enough to support decision-making and control loops, often under constraints of limited onboard computing and bandwidth.
In this work, these challenges are examined through vision and LiDAR detection pipelines implemented on the Minion sensing platform.

The central focus of this investigation is the development of a late-fusion framework that integrates information from both sensing domains to enhance detection robustness and confidence.
This framework represents the primary contribution of the research, demonstrating how visual and LiDAR detections can be combined at the decision level to leverage the complementary strengths of each sensing modality.% while mitigating their individual limitations.

To establish a meaningful basis for evaluation, two single-modality detection systems were selected as comparative baselines.
YOLOv8 exemplifies data-driven neural network detectors optimized for vision-based perception, and was selected for its status as one of the most widely adopted vision-based object detection methods.
GB-CACHE is a deterministic algorithm designed for LiDAR-based object detection and classification.

% These two approaches represent two distinct strategies for real-time perception, and the late-fusion method developed in this work integrates their complementary strengths to achieve higher confidence detections across a range of operating conditions.
Each of these models was selected for its robust, real-time capabilities and to provide a clear contrast between neural network and deterministic approaches. 
This chapter examines both models, detailing its methods, training, efficiency, detection, and classification results.
Section~\ref{yolo} presents the YOLOv8 vision-based detector and its implementation for real-time object recognition.
Section~\ref{gbcache} describes the GB-CACHE algorithm for LiDAR-based clustering and object segmentation.
Finally, this research combines the decisions from each of these detection modes in a late-fusion framework in Section~\ref{fusion} and evaluates the performance of fused results relative to the single-modality detectors through a comparative analysis of detection accuracy, computational efficiency, and real-time operation.

\paragraph{real-Time Performance}

To ensure a consistent and meaningful comparison between the two real-time detection pipelines, runtime was selected as the primary performance metric. 
Processor and memory utilization were not chosen because the two methods rely on fundamentally different hardware and memory architectures: the neural network–based YOLO executes on the \ac{GPU} with high-bandwidth \ac{vRAM}, whereas GB-CACHE operates entirely on the \ac{CPU} and conventional system memory and significantly lower memory bandwidth.
To be effective for real-time use, both pipelines must effectively process data faster than they receive it.
This performance evaluation of both detectors was conducted using all 98 ROS bag files containing synchronized camera and LiDAR data on Minion's hardware. Data was played back at real-time speeds using the ROS parameter \texttt{use\_sim\_time = true}.

\section{YOLO} \label{yolo}

% Purpose and Context
The eighth generation of the \ac{YOLO} framework was selected to provide a baseline performance for real-time vision-based detection in this research. 
This method of detection gained popularity for its ease of use and implementation for detecting and localizing objects within an image.
Once an object is detected, the pixel coordinates it occupies within the image frame are bounded with a rectangle known as the bounding box (see Figure \ref{fig:YOLO_tower}).
Its open-source models are pretrained on the COCO dataset, a large repository containing approximately 1.5 million labeled images, and are provided in various sizes and qualities, which can be deployed on a wide variety of edge computing hardware.
Additionally, transfer learning is simple to apply, allowing these models to be tailored for more specific use cases, such as maritime environments.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Images_old/YOLO_ex1.png}
    \caption{Rectangular bounding boxes placed around maritime objects detected by a re-trained YOLO detection model.}
    \label{fig:YOLO_tower}
\end{figure}

The name YOLO refers to the single-shot \ac{CNN} architecture, which performs both detection and localization in a single pass.
While this design was key to achieving real-time detection speeds, the accuracy of an object’s bounding box within an image was limited by the available training data. 
Prior to the release of version 8 in 2023, the model required that the center and bounds of each detected object be explicitly encoded within the model, requiring a large corpus of labeled data for training.
Version 8 introduced dual detection heads at the end of the CNN architecture: the first head identifies if and where an object is present within the image, while the second head determines the object's bounding box.
This “anchor-free” architectural change not only improves the reliability of object localization but also reduces the burden of transfer learning required for fine-tuning pre-trained models in earlier versions \cite{terven2023, ultralytics}.

The widespread adoption and speed of YOLOv8 make it the optimal choice for providing a baseline visual detection method, and the fundamental architectural changes in this version validate its selection for a late-fusion approach.

\subsection{Training Data} \label{sec:yolo_training_data}

A manually labeled image dataset was constructed to support the transfer learning and evaluation of the YOLO object detection network. 
All YOLO training images were obtained from video frames that are not present within the rosbag data.
The HDR camera recorded and streamed data continuously throughout each test and competition day. 
Due to the rate of data accumulation within each rosbag, as described in section~\ref{sec:data_overview}, synchronized LiDAR, camera, and ROS data were recorded intermittently and strategically.  
This ensured that a large pool of video data remained separate from the main dataset and available for YOLO training.

For this research, YOLOv8 was trained on a custom maritime dataset derived from sensor recordings collected aboard the Minion platform.
The dataset includes labeled examples of navigation markers, buoys, vessels, and other relevant maritime objects.
The final dataset contained 778 annotated images and 1934 labeled maritime objects.

\subsection{Results} \label{sec:yolo_results}

The fine-tuned YOLOv8-s model achieved a balance of accuracy and speed.
Performance metrics include \ac{mAP}, precision-recall curves, and confusion matrices for key object classes.
Performance metrics, including mean average precision (mAP), classification fidelity, and computational efficiency, validate the model’s selection and real-time capabilities on the Minion platform.

The training process for the YOLOv8-s model, detailed in subplots of Figure~\ref{fig:yolo_training_curve}, confirms successful adaptation via transfer learning. 

\paragraph{Training and Validation Loss}
Figure~\ref{fig:yolo_training_curve} shows loss curves related to bounding boxes (\texttt{box\_loss}), class labels(\texttt{cls\_loss}), and distribution focal loss (\texttt{train/dfl\_loss}) for both training (outlined in blue) and cross-validation (outlined in red).
The training loss curves show stable, consistent convergence across all three components throughout training.
The \texttt{train/box\_loss} metric decreases smoothly to a value below 0.6, indicating that the model is training effectively over time as the predicted bounding boxes become more aligned with the ground-truth labels. 
The classification loss (\texttt{train/cls\_loss}) drops rapidly during the early epochs and then tapers off, reflecting the model's ability to easily distinguish between the five training classes. 
The Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) (\texttt{train/dfl\_loss}) governs the probabilistic location of box edges, and unsurprisingly, has a similar trend as \texttt{box\_loss} with a final value near 0.8. 
The downward trends across all three metrics demonstrate that the model steadily improves in both coarse and fine localization and in class prediction accuracy as the number of training epochs increases.
All three validation loss curves closely mirror the training losses appearing directly above each subplot.

\paragraph{Precision, Recall, and mAP}
The remaining four detection-quality metrics, outlined in yellow in Figure~\ref{fig:yolo_training_curve}, detail the precision, recall, and \ac{mAP} of the transfer-learning.
Precision increases rapidly during early epochs, shows some instability between epochs 40 and 65, and finally stabilizes at a value close to 0.8. 
Recall steadily improves to about 0.8, as well.
Together, these metrics indicate that the model's class prediction has a much higher true-positive rate than false positives or false negatives, at approximately 4 to 1.
The mAP50 metric measures true-positive detections at an \ac{IoU} threshold of 0.50 and rises consistently toward 0.80, reflecting strong combined improvements in both classification and bounding-box localization. The stricter mAP50-95 metric also rises smoothly, indicating that the model is not only detecting objects but also producing increasingly accurate bounding boxes across a range of \ac{IoU} thresholds. 

The close correspondence between these metrics and the validation loss curves also indicates that the model has not over-trained. Training and validation losses for box regression, classification, and DFL decrease together with minimal separation, and none of the validation metrics exhibit late-epoch degradation. This alignment shows that improvements made during training generalize to unseen data rather than reflecting memorization of the training set.

\section{GB-CACHE} \label{gbcache}
%\subfile{sections/gbcache}

Grid-Based Clustered Adaptive Concave Hull Extraction (GB-CACHE) is a spatial indexing and clustering methodology specifically designed for efficient processing of three-dimensional point cloud data in maritime environments \cite{coyleE}. 
The algorithm provides rapid object segmentation through grid-based spatial partitioning followed by concave-hull boundary extraction, enabling real-time detection performance essential for autonomous navigation safety.

\subsection{Grid-Based Spatial Indexing and Clustering} \label{gbcache_clustering}
% ### Grid-Based Spatial Indexing and Clustering

The fundamental principle underlying GB-CACHE is to partition the three-dimensional LiDAR point cloud into a regular grid, facilitating efficient spatial queries and proximity-based clustering. Rather than evaluating pairwise distances among all points, the grid-based approach achieves linear complexity by organizing points into fixed spatial bins. Each grid cell represents a discrete volumetric region, and points falling within the same cell or adjacent cells are considered spatially proximate.
This spatial indexing approach proves particularly advantageous for maritime LiDAR data. 
The sparse nature of maritime point clouds enables the grid structure to efficiently isolate regions containing potential objects while discarding empty space, thereby reducing both memory consumption and computational overhead.

% ### Concave Hull Extraction and Feature Calculation
\subsection{Concave Hull and Feature Extraction} \label{gbcache_features}

Within the GB CACHE framework, each newly detected cluster is assigned a sequentially incremented object ID. 
This ID persists as long as the object remains continuously within the autonomous system's field of view.
However, if an object leaves the sensor’s field of view due to occlusion or other factors and later reappears, it is assigned a new object ID upon re-detection. IDs are also not guaranteed to be retained across separate ROS bag files or after a restart of the GB-CACHE node.

For each new object identified, a concave hull is created to define the object's spatial extent. 
While convex hulls guarantee computational efficiency and provide simple bounding representations, concave hulls more accurately capture object shape by allowing inward boundary curvature. 
This distinction proves critical for maritime objects with non-convex geometries, including U-shaped channel markers or vessels with significant superstructure features.

The concave hull generation process balances boundary accuracy against computational cost through a configurable complexity parameter that controls the degree of boundary concavity. 
The resulting polygon provides a compact geometric representation suitable for both visualization and feature extraction for subsequent classification. 
Beyond simple bounding boxes, concave hull boundaries enable the calculation of geometric features, such as area and perimeter, that support object classification. 

The algorithmic design prioritizes computational efficiency to enable real-time processing on autonomous surface vessel hardware with limited computing resources. 
The processing pipeline operates incrementally on each LiDAR scan, maintaining no temporal history and requiring no iterative refinement. 
This stateless design simplifies implementation and guarantees bounded worst-case execution time, a critical property for safety-critical autonomous systems. 
Memory requirements scale with grid dimensions rather than point count, providing predictable resource consumption independent of scene complexity.

\subsection{Training Data} \label{training_data_gbcache}

Training data for the GB-CACHE's \ac{MGC} were obtained from the rosbag dataset described in Section~\ref{sec:data_overview}.

In general, each class of object was observed in a stationary position at a range of 40 to 60 meters as the Minion \ac{USV} approached the object to a range of 5 meters. 
In total, this results in approximately 20-30 seconds of training data per object class.
Notably, training for the Jon boat class was performed differently due to time constraints for data collection.
In this case, Minion remained stationary, and the Jon boat slowly approached from a similar range and rate.

% Rosbags containing training data for GB-CACHE were 
% within the dataset was recorded for training each of the six object classes, and these bag IDs are listed in Table \textcolor{red}{insert table}.

The training bagfiles are post-processed with MATLAB. 
For each object class, a manually identified object ID is selected within a bag file over the desired observation interval. 
This ID is used to extract the corresponding metadata from the \texttt{obj\_w\_class} topic for that time period. 
This metadata is processed to extract 10 classification features, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:gbcache_features}.
Features are generally split into characteristics of LiDAR return intensity from the raw point cloud and geometric features derived from the extracted convex hull and the occupation grid density.
These measurements summarize the object's size, aspect ratio, spatial distribution, and structural characteristics, providing a compact numerical description suitable for downstream classification. The resulting vector of ten features is stored for each of the six object classes and serves as input to the \ac{MGC} process.

\subsection{Results} \label{sec:gbcache_results}

To evaluate computational performance, the GB-CACHE processing sequence is grouped into three subroutines.
These subroutines correspond to the sequential stages that handle LiDAR data input, spatial filtering, and map updating during each processing cycle.
The first subroutine, ``Filter Points'', removes points reflected from the water's surface, reducing noise and false positives with object detection.
The second routine, ``Add Points'', assigns the remaining raw LiDAR returns to the occupancy grid, \textcolor{blue}{listing them as unprocessed}.
The final subroutine, ``Map Points'', combines segmentation, clustering, and classification.
Figure~\ref{fig:gbcache_flow_analysis} provides a block diagram of the GB-CACHE workflow with these three subroutines windowing the related tasks.

To assess performance, all ROS bags were replayed on the same Minion PC hardware that recorded them, providing only the raw LiDAR data and navigational topics to the GB-CACHE ROS node. 
This approach perfectly simulates the operational data processing environment. 
The execution time of each subroutine was measured along with the number of points processed by each function. 
Overall, more than 1.7 million executions of ``Filter Points'' and ``Add Points'' and more than 45 thousand executions of "Update Map" were measured across the entire dataset.

The LiDAR data is transmitted at 100 Hz from three Livox Horizon units, while the ROS topic \texttt{Obj\_w\_class} generated by GB-CACHE is published at 5 Hz. 
The first two subroutines examined, ``Filter Points'' and ``Add Points'', execute sequentially without parallelization. 
These processes must handle raw data from all three LiDAR units at 100 Hz; therefore, the summed total execution time of these two subroutines must not exceed 3.33 milliseconds.
The ``Update Map'' subroutine receives the outputs of ``Filter Points'' and ``Add Points'' but runs asynchronously and outputs detections only at 5 Hz, providing an allotted execution time of 200 milliseconds per cycle.

\subsection{MGC Feature Analysis} \label{features_gbcache}

The classification of objects utilizes an \ac{MGC} framework to model the joint probability distribution of extracted features for each object class. 
Under the assumption that features follow multivariate Gaussian distributions within each class, the classifier computes class likelihoods using covariance matrices and mean vectors estimated from labeled training data. 
This approach offers computational efficiency during inference while capturing correlations among features, thereby enhancing discriminative power.

% For a feature vector representing an observed object, the classifier evaluates the probability density under each class-specific Gaussian model and assigns the class yielding maximum posterior probability. 
% This framework naturally accommodates multiple object classes and provides confidence estimates through the posterior probability values.
The Gaussian assumption simplifies the classification problem to estimating a probability density function for each class from the feature distributions within the training samples and applying Bayes' rule during inference to select the most probable class.
This framework naturally accommodates multiple object classes and provides confidence estimates through the posterior probability values.

To evaluate both the discriminative power and redundancy within the ten-feature set, two complementary analyses were conducted. 
First, the \ac{MI} between each feature and the class label was examined to quantify feature relevance. 
Second, \ac{PMI} was evaluated across all feature combinations to identify redundancy within the set.
Finally, the \ac{PMI} was used to determine an optimal set of the ten features.

\paragraph{Mutual Information}
\Ac{MI} quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about one variable given knowledge of another. 

First, the \ac{MI} between each continuous feature $X$ and the discrete class label $Y$ was computed to determine how informative that feature is for distinguishing between object classes. 
This calculation measures how strongly variations in each feature reduce uncertainty in the associated class label and therefore reflects each feature's individual contribution to object identification. 
For a continuous variable $X$ and a discrete variable $Y$, mutual information is computed by first discretizing $X$ using histogram binning ($N=20$ bins) to approximate its continuous distribution as a discrete probability mass function. 
The \ac{MI} between the discretized feature and class label is then computed as:
\begin{equation}
I(X;Y)
= \sum_{x \in X} \sum_{y \in Y} 
p(x,y) \,
\log \left( \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} \right).
\end{equation}
where $p(x,y)$ is the joint probability mass function and $p(x)$, $p(y)$ are the marginal distributions.
These results are presented in Figure~\ref{MGC_feature_analysis1}.
The feature ranking by mutual information with class labels revealed substantial variation in discriminative power across the ten features, as shown in Figure~\ref{MGC_feature_analysis2}. 
The top four features ($f_3, f_4, f_5, f_6$) exhibited \ac{MI} values ranging from 0.59 to 0.64 bits, providing nearly twice the amount of discriminative information for determining the correct object label as the other six.
These features appear to capture fundamental geometric properties that distinguish maritime object classes. 
Features ($f_8, f_{10}, f_9$) exhibited moderate \ac{MI} values (between 0.18 and 0.30 bits), suggesting practical but less critical discriminative power. 
Finally, the bottom three features ($f_7, f_1, f_2$) demonstrated weak relationships with class labels.

\paragraph{Feature redundancy}
Following the feature-class analysis, PMI was computed to identify redundant measurements between pairs of features.
For each pair of features ($X_i$, $X_j$), the \ac{MI} quantifies the amount of shared information between them.
High \ac{MI} values indicate that one feature provides little additional information beyond what the other already captures.
Since all features are continuous variables, both features in each pair were discretized using histogram binning ($N=20$ bins) before computing \ac{MI}. 
\begin{equation}
I(X_i; X_j)
= \sum_{x_i} \sum_{x_j}
p(x_i, x_j) \,
\log \left( \frac{p(x_i, x_j)}{p(x_i)\,p(x_j)} \right).
\end{equation}
These results are presented as a $10 \times 10$ matrix, where each element $I(X_i; X_j)$ represents the mutual information between features $i$ and $j$ in Figure~\ref{MGC_feature_analysis_3}.
The most extreme redundancy is observed in the geometric feature cluster ($f_3, f_4, f_5$), where the pairwise mutual information exceeds 1.4 bits for all three combinations. 
This indicates that these features capture highly overlapping geometric information about object extent or boundary characteristics. 
Similarly, features $f_1$ and $f_2$ exhibit a strong correlation (1.28 bits), suggesting redundant intensity measurements. 
The redundancy analysis confirmed that opportunities exist for dimensionality reduction without necessarily sacrificing classification performance.



\paragraph{Optimal Feature Subsets}
Following the redundancy analysis, the minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) criterion was employed to identify optimal feature subsets. 
The mRMR framework is a feature selection methodology that prioritizes variables that are highly relevant to class labels, as measured by mutual information, while simultaneously minimizing redundancy among selected features. 
This optimization procedure ensures that the resulting subset consists of features that each contribute novel discriminative information, providing a compact and practical basis for classification.

The mRMR algorithm uses a greedy forward selection approach, initialized with feature $f_3$, which exhibited the highest feature-class mutual information (0.6430 bits). At each subsequent iteration, the remaining candidate features are scored according to
\begin{equation}
\text{mRMR}(f_k) = I(f_k; Y) - \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{f_i \in S} I(f_k; f_i)
\end{equation}
where $I(f_k; Y)$ represents the relevance of candidate feature $f_k$ to the class labels, $S$ is the set of already-selected features, and the summation term quantifies the average redundancy between $f_k$ and the selected subset. 
The feature with the highest mRMR score is added to $S$, and the process repeats until all features are added.
The optimal subset size was determined by identifying the point of diminishing returns in the mRMR score curve.

% Table~\ref{tbl:mrmr_selection} shows the sequential feature selection process and the mRMR score at each step.

The mRMR scores decline after the second feature, and become negative by the fourth feature selection. 
This indicates that beyond the first two features, additional features contribute more redundancy than discriminative value. 
The analysis identified an optimal feature subset of just two features: $f_3$ (MI $= 0.6430$) and $f_8$ (MI $= 0.2990$). 
This dramatic reduction from ten to two features suggests that the majority of class-discriminative information is captured by a small number of well-chosen measurements.

\section{Late Fusion} \label{fusion}
\label{fusion_method}
Having established the capabilities and limitations of both camera-based and LiDAR-based detection systems in maritime environments, this section describes the implementation of a late fusion method that combines their outputs at the decision level. 
The goal is to use the demonstrated strengths of each system to improve detection accuracy while maintaining the real-time computational performance of both sensing modes. 

\subsection{Fusion Reference Frame Selection}
The proposed approach utilizes a Kalman filter on the independent object detections generated by YOLO and GB-CACHE following classification.
This allows for the fused detection label to be influenced by each model proportionate to the level of confidence we have in either.
The image frame is used for late fusion to avoid unnecessary computational overhead and preserve real-time functionality, as well as to minimize the accumulation of error in the data through repeated transformations.
YOLOv8 detections are produced natively in image coordinates, requiring no transformation for fusion in this frame. 
Furthermore, vision-based detections inherently lack the three-dimensional spatial information necessary for accurate placement in the world frame without additional information.
In contrast, GB-CACHE produces detections in the map frame with three-dimensional spatial information, which is then projected into the image frame through the extrinsic and intrinsic transformations established during sensor calibration. 
This unidirectional projection from LiDAR to image coordinates helps minimize the accumulation of error that may otherwise result from bidirectional transformations. 
By selecting the image frame as the fusion reference, the methodology preserves the native accuracy of vision-based detections while incorporating spatial information from LiDAR through a well-characterized geometric transformation.
However, it is recognized that this choice may introduce a bias in favor of the YOLO method.
With the fusion reference frame established, the next step is to express both detection sources in a common bounding-box representation suitable for association and filtering.


\subsection{Projection and ROI Construction}

The fusion pipeline activates each time an image is received within the ROS operating system. 
The YOLO detection method independently produces a detection for each timestamped image. 
Concurrently, the detections produced by GB-CACHE are queried to locate any objects within the 65-degree horizontal field of view in front of Minion at the time the image was taken. 
The orientation of Minion is interpolated backwards to the timestamp of the image frame, and this transform, along with the other transforms in the transform tree, is used to project the polygon, which represents the concave hull of each object into the image frame, along with its associated height. 
The vertices of this polygon are all converted to x, y pixel coordinates in the image frame, and the maximum and minimum of these values are used to determine the bounding box of the classified object. 
This process yields a YOLO-provided ROI and a LiDAR-derived ROI expressed in the same pixel-based coordinate system for each candidate detection.

Because GB CACHE is built on an occupancy grid, each LiDAR return is represented by a cell rather than an exact point. 
This structure creates an inherent spatial uncertainty. 
Any object mapped to the grid can lie anywhere within the cell that contains its points.
The resulting map frame error is determined by the grid's 100 mm cell size.
Each detected point has a maximum spatial uncertainty of $\pm 50$ mm in both horizontal and vertical directions. 
This error varies with the orientation of the minion relative to the grid, and has maximum error at $n$ intervals of $\theta = n\pi / 2 + \pi/4$. 
When this fixed-world-space error is projected through the camera intrinsics into the image frame, it becomes a pixel error that varies with distance.
This behavior follows directly from the geometry of the pinhole camera model, described in Section~\ref{img_tform}.
Similarly, the YOLO method also has a pixel error due to the image scaling applied to feed its network; however, this error is bounded by the scale factor and so does not change with object distance.

By plotting the theoretical maximum errors for both methods, we can determine the threshold at which the GB-CACHE method is more accurate than YOLO. This results of this analysis is prsented in Figure~\ref{fig:theory_error_1}. 
Here we see that GB-CACHE should achieve greater accuracy in the horizontal axis near 28.8 meters, and the vertical axis at 44.2 meters.

\subsection{Detection Association}

YOLO detection labels are compared with those from GB-CACHE to determine objects of the same class. 
The \ac{IoU} of these \ac{ROI}s are examined, and those meeting an \ac{IoU}  threshold of 0.7 are then processed with the fusion algorithm. 
If multiple candidates meet these criteria, pairs with the highest IoU are selected, and the center-to-center distance is used to break any ties.
If either of these conditions fails, the labels used for this method are still available for other system tasks.
YOLO is unable to spatially condition its detections to the desired range of 60 meters; therefore, single-mode detections are ignored.
This rule-based association framework permits graceful degradation in scenarios where one sensing modality fails or the spatial calibration or temporal synchronization becomes unreliable.

\subsection{Offline Confidence Weighting}

The reliability of each sensing modality is characterized offline using a static Kalman filter formulation. 
During calibration, only detection pairs with ground-truth ROIs are used to estimate the per-edge error statistics for YOLO and GB-CACHE. 
These statistics determine the confidence weights applied during online fusion.

Each ROI pair that passes into the late-fusion pipeline is defined by four pixel coordinates.
These  correspond to the left, right, top, and bottom edges of the bounding box, and are the primary states of the implemented Kalman filter $X$,
\begin{equation}
X_{S} = (L_{S}, R_{S}, T_{S}, B_{S}),
\end{equation}

where $S \in \{\text{LiDAR}, \text{YOLO}\}$ denotes the source. 
Therefore, the error between $X_{S}$ and a set of ground-truth ROI labels $X_{GT}$ is defined
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}_{S} = X_{S} - X_{\text{GT}}
\end{equation}

By assuming that the distributions of errors $\mathbf{E}_S$ will trend towards a normal distribution  ($\mathcal{N}(\mu , \sigma^2)$) for a sufficiently large set of samples $N$, the error for each state in $X$ is characterized as  
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\mu} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} E_{i}\\
\mathbf{\sigma}^2 &= \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl(E_{i} - \mu\bigr)^2
\end{align}

Although the errors are not guaranteed to be strictly Gaussian, inspection across the dataset revealed unimodal and approximately symmetric error profiles, supporting the use of the normal-error model for variance-based weighting.
Under this assumption, a vector of the variance for each state $\vec{\sigma}^{2}_{S}$ provides a direct measure of reliability for any detection source $S$.
Sources with less variance are more consistent with ground truth and should receive a higher weight in the fused estimate, and are generally calculated as
\begin{equation}
w = \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Kalman Weighted Fusion}

Because temporal propagation is not required for this application, the Kalman filter is applied in its static form, where the fused measurement is the variance-weighted combination of two independent observations.
Finally, the bounds of a fused ROI can be expressed as a weighted average between two detection modes $S_1$ and $S_2$ with the equation
\begin{equation}
X_{\text{fused}} =
\frac{
(X_{S_1}-\bar{\mu}_{S_1}) \, \bar{w}_{S_2}
+
(X_{S_2}-\bar{\mu}_{S_2}) \, \bar{w}_{X,S_1}
}{
\bar{w}_{X,S_1} + \bar{w}_{S_2}
}
\label{eq:fused_edge}
\end{equation}

where $\bar{w}_{S}$ and $\bar{\mu}_{S}$ are vector of weights $w$ and mean errors $\mu$ determined for each state of $X$, and subtracting the mean error $\mu$ compensates for the bias observed in the training dataset.

\chapter{Image Transform} \label{img_tform}
% \subfile{sections/img_tform}

To obtain the two-dimensional pixel coordinates of the distorted image ($u, v$) in the camera frame $C$ from a three-dimensional coordinate ($X_{L}, Y_L, Z_L$) in the LiDAR frame $L$:

% 3D LiDAR point to distorted image pixel
% \paragraph{Projection from LiDAR to image.}
Let $\mathbf{R}\in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$, $\mathbf{t}\in \mathbb{R}^{3\times1}$, $_L\mathbf{P} = [X_L, Y_L, Z_L, 1]^T$,  and $_C\mathbf{P} = \;_{C}^{L}\mathbf{T} \; _L\mathbf{P}$, then:
\begin{equation*}
_C\mathbf{P} = [X_C, Y_C, Z_C, 1]^T \;= \; _{C}^{L}\mathbf{T} \;[X_L, Y_L, Z_L, 1]^T,
% ,\qquad
% _{C}^{L}\mathbf{T} =
% \begin{bmatrix}
% \mathbf{R}_{C}^{L} & \mathbf{t}_{C}^{L} \\
% \mathbf{0}^T & 1
% \end{bmatrix}.
% \label{eq:LiDAR_to_cam_frame}
\end{equation*}

such that:
\begin{equation*}
    _{L}^{C}\mathbf{T} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        _{L}^{C}\mathbf{R} & _{L}^{C}\mathbf{t} \\
        \mathbf{0}^\mathrm{T} & 1
    \end{bmatrix},
\end{equation*}

We obtain the pixel coordinates ($x_n, y_n$) in the undistorted image frame by pinhole projection:
\begin{equation*}
x_n = \frac{X_C}{Z_C}, \qquad
y_n = \frac{Y_C}{Z_C}, \qquad
r^2 = x_n^2 + y_n^2 .
\label{eq:normalized_coords}
\end{equation*}

Apply the radial distortion model:
\begin{align*}
r^2 & = x_n^2 + y_n^2 .\\
x' & = x_n\!\left(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6\right), \\
y' & = y_n\!\left(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6\right).
\label{eq:radial_distortion_apply}
\end{align*}

Map ($x', y'$) to distorted pixel coordinates ($u,v$) using the intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
f_x & s & c_x \\
0   & f_y & c_y \\
0   & 0   & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
% \mathbf{K} =
% \begin{bmatrix}
% f_x & s & c_x \\
% 0   & f_y & c_y \\
% 0   & 0   & 1
% \end{bmatrix},
% \quad
\Rightarrow\ 
\begin{cases}
u = f_x x' + s y' + c_x,\\
v = f_y y' + c_y.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:intrinsics_to_pixels}
\end{equation}
