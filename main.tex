This research presents a quantitative performance comparison between \ac{LiDAR} and vision-based sensing for real-time maritime object detection on autonomous surface vessels. Using \acposs{ERAU} Minion platform and 2024 Maritime RobotX Challenge data, this study evaluates the detection of six maritime object categories using two representative models. YOLOv8 provides a neural network vision-based method, and GB-CACHE provides a deterministic LiDAR-based method. Both models have been previously demonstrated to run in real time on \acp{USV}.
The evaluation methodology encompasses multi-sensor calibration, real-time performance analysis, and the introduction of a late-fusion strategy in the image frame to reduce bounding-box uncertainty. 
Performance metrics include training requirements, precision, recall, \ac{mAP}, and computational efficiency. 
YOLO achieves low-latency, high-mAP visual detection, while GB-CACHE provides deterministic, CPU-level runtime guarantees with high geometric accuracy, and the Kalman weighted fusion guarantees an improvement in the bounds of the region of interest.

\chapter{Introduction} \label{introduction}

The rise of autonomous systems is transforming numerous industries, and the maritime sector is no exception. Uncrewed surface vessels have the potential to improve safety and operational efficiency by performing the dull, dirty, and dangerous tasks that have traditionally required human operators~\cite{bae2023}. Their effectiveness depends on environmental situational awareness, which refers to the system’s ability to perceive and interpret relevant features of the surrounding environment. Real-time object detection and classification provide the foundation for this capability and are essential for safe navigation and informed decision making in complex maritime settings.

Although the automotive industry has achieved considerable advancement in autonomous perception~\cite{cui2022}, comparable progress for maritime platforms remains limited. Studies in ground vehicle autonomy outnumber maritime autonomy publications by nearly an order of magnitude, and a similar disparity exists in object detection research~\cite{bae2023}. While certain perception strategies from automotive systems can be adapted, the maritime domain presents fundamentally different challenges~\cite{prasad2017}. Marine environments are unstructured and dynamic, with object densities that range from sparse to highly congested, particularly in littoral and harbor regions where uncrewed vessels frequently operate.

Maritime autonomy requires high-fidelity sensing to identify and track objects within the vessel’s operational environment. The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea \ac{COLREGs} specify the shapes, colors, and light patterns used to communicate vessel status and navigational intent, which means that autonomous systems must correctly interpret these visual and structural cues to operate safely \cite{COLREGs1972}. Objects must therefore be accurately located and identified so that an uncrewed surface vessel can make informed decisions and maintain safe navigation. Marine radar lacks the spatial resolution needed at the close ranges required for maneuvering tasks, so uncrewed maritime systems rely on optical and \ac{LiDAR} sensors~\cite{clunie2021}. Both sensing modalities face significant challenges in marine conditions. Cameras provide rich color and texture information needed to identify navigation aids and visually distinctive targets, yet their performance degrades under haze, fog, glare, or variable illumination~\cite{prasad2017}. Furthermore, camera imagery inherently lacks spatial
depth unless supported by additional sensing or estimation methods. \ac{LiDAR} provides accurate three-dimensional structure but does not capture the spectral detail necessary for interpreting color-coded markers or visually encoded cues~\cite{roriz2022}. These complementary limitations motivate the use of sensor fusion to integrate the strengths of both modalities~\cite{haghbayan2018}.

Real-time fusion is particularly important for embedded maritime platforms, where computational resources are limited and operational demands are high~\cite{thompson2023}. Efficient fusion frameworks support low-latency, high-accuracy perception under size, weight, and power constraints and enable mission profiles that require precise situational awareness. These include navigation through littoral zones, search and rescue operations, scientific surveys, and defense-oriented missions.

\section{Problem Statement}

The central problem addressed by this dissertation is the lack of quantitative, empirical evidence comparing real-time \ac{LiDAR} and vision-based object detection systems in maritime environments, combined with the absence of validated real-time fusion strategies capable of improving detection accuracy beyond single-modality methods while operating within the computational limits of embedded uncrewed surface platforms.

Researchers in maritime perception have primarily focused on vision or \ac{LiDAR} modalities individually due to the limited availability of multimodal datasets. Many studies rely on heterogeneous data and inconsistent evaluation protocols, and few perform rigorous comparisons of identical object classes~\cite{zhang2021}. Although late fusion is commonly proposed as a means of improving robustness~\cite{farahnakian2020}, its computational cost and real-time behavior have not been consistently characterized for uncrewed maritime systems~\cite{haghbayan2018}. Reports on spatial calibration and temporal synchronization vary widely in scope and detail~\cite{westenberger2011}, which undermines reproducibility and makes cross-study comparisons difficult~\cite{su2023}. This dissertation addresses these gaps by presenting a unified experimental framework for evaluating both detection modalities and introducing a reproducible late-fusion approach supported by validated spatial and temporal calibration procedures.

\section{Research Objectives}

Using data collected over multiple days in conditions ranging from near-optimal daylight to overcast, this research examines surface-level maritime perception and will ultimately:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Develop spatial and temporal calibration techniques for synchronizing asynchronous camera and \ac{LiDAR} data streams.
    \item Characterize the performance differential between vision-based and LiDAR-based object detection systems through quantitative analysis of detection accuracy and computational efficiency.
    \item Analyze the features extracted for LiDAR-based object classification for their discriminative value and redundancy.
    \item Implement a method that fuses decision-level information from the vision and LiDAR-based detection methods to demonstrate performance improvements over single-mode approaches.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodological Overview}

To accomplish these objectives, a modular perception system was developed and deployed on ERAU’s uncrewed surface research platform, Minion (see Figure~\ref{fig:minion}). The system integrates multiple cameras and \ac{LiDAR} sensors with validated spatial and temporal calibration procedures. A comprehensive dataset was collected during the 2024 Maritime RobotX Challenge~\cite{lachguar} and through dedicated training missions, focusing on six classes of maritime objects. Data was collected in both open water and controlled marine environments under favorable weather and lighting conditions. These controlled conditions ensured safety for the ERAU team and provided a consistent baseline for analysis. More challenging environments, including nighttime operations, fog, rain, and rough seas, are left for future research.

All data were recorded and analyzed within the ROS Noetic framework to ensure consistent timing and reproducible evaluation across modalities and fusion configurations.

It is important to clarify that this study is not intended to highlight obvious modality-specific limitations, such as the reduced performance of vision-based systems at night, nor to evaluate every available detection method. Instead, the aim is to provide a controlled and consistent comparison between a nondeterministic neural network–based vision detector and a deterministic LiDAR-based method. YOLOv8 was selected as the representative deep learning model due to its widespread adoption and strong performance in real-time vision tasks~\cite{terven2023}. GB-CACHE was selected for \ac{LiDAR} detection because it offers transparency, reproducibility, and deterministic behavior, which are desirable characteristics for safety-critical maritime systems~\cite{coyleE}.
First, comparing two neural network-based approaches would not provide a meaningful comparison between visual
and LiDAR sensing modalities. Second, it is difficult to enforce strict rules governing
system behavior when using neural network methods. This becomes a liability when
performing high-stakes or other operational tasks that require consistent, predictable
behavior. Deterministic methods, by contrast, operate according to explicitly defined rules
by design, making their investigation highly relevant to the situational awareness of
uncrewed vessels. This method has demonstrated its capability for real-time use on
Minion, and its geometric, rules-based approach makes it both transparent and
reproducible. These characteristics position GB-CACHE as well-suited to the sensing
requirements of uncrewed maritime systems, as well as a strong deterministic counterpart
to the neural network-based YOLO method for vision.

Real-time perception for autonomous surface vessels has evolved substantially over the past decade, primarily driven by advances in deep learning architectures and sensor technologies. %initially developed for terrestrial autonomous vehicles.
Vision-based detection methods, particularly single-stage architectures such as YOLO~\cite{redmon2016}, demonstrate strong performance on automotive benchmarks~\cite{geiger2013} but experience degradation under extreme illumination variations.
\ac{LiDAR}-based approaches offer complementary capabilities through geometric precision independent of illumination~\cite{zhou2018}.
Recent surveys of maritime autonomy~\cite{bae2023, zhang2021, xue2025, ferreira2022, bai2022} reinforce perception as a critical enabling technology, yet systematic performance characterization of these methods in maritime operational conditions remains limited.

The maritime environment presents distinct challenges compared to terrestrial scenarios.
Studies of visual detection in coastal and harbor settings report substantial performance degradation due to water reflections, high dynamic range, and low-texture backgrounds~\cite{prasad2017}.
\ac{LiDAR} systems face complementary difficulties with sparse water-surface returns and atmospheric scattering~\cite{kunz2005}.
While sensor fusion has been proposed to address individual modality limitations~\cite{huang2024, liang2022}, most fusion research focuses on automotive applications using terrestrial datasets such as KITTI~\cite{geiger2013} or Waymo \cite{sun2020}, and its direct applicability to maritime platforms operating under power and computational constraints has not been thoroughly validated~\cite{wang2020a}.

Autonomous surface vessels must operate in compliance with the \ac{COLREGs}~\cite{COLREGs1972}, which define navigation rules governing vessel interactions, right-of-way obligations, and safe maneuvering.
These rules implicitly require reliable detection and classification of other vessels, navigation markers, and obstacles under diverse environmental conditions.

In addition, the \ac{IMO} is developing a regulatoryframework for \ac{MASS}. 
Draft provisions for a future \ac{MASS} Code are under review by the Maritime Safety Committee, and as of 2025 no binding regulatory standard is in force \cite{IMO_MASS_RSE_2021}. 
These regulatory efforts underscore the importance of perception systems capable of supporting safe, compliant, and responsible autonomous operation.

This review synthesizes research across vision and \ac{LiDAR}-based detection methods independently before addressing multimodal fusion architectures and calibration requirements, and concludes by identifying specific research gaps motivating this dissertation's contributions.

\section{Deep Learning Foundations and Single-Stage Detectors}

The application of \acp{CNN} to visual recognition gained widespread adoption following Krizhevsky et al.'s demonstration that deep architectures trained on large-scale datasets could achieve superior performance on ImageNet~\cite{krizhevsky2017}.
Subsequent architectural innovations, including residual learning frameworks~\cite{he2016}, demonstrated that network depth correlates with representational capacity for complex visual recognition tasks.
Early object detection methods adapted these classification networks into two-stage pipelines.
Girshick et al.~\cite{girshick2014} introduced \ac{R-CNN}, which applies convolutional neural networks to region proposals; however, the computational overhead limited throughput.
Ren et al.~\cite{ren2016} addressed these limitations with Faster \ac{R-CNN}, introducing region proposal networks that enabled end-to-end training and substantially improved inference speed.

Single-stage detectors emerged to address latency constraints by reformulating detection as direct regression from image features to object classes and bounding box coordinates.
The \ac{YOLO} family exemplifies this paradigm, enabling real-time operation through unified spatial prediction~\cite{redmon2016}.
Subsequent \ac{YOLO} iterations introduced architectural refinements, including multi-scale detection heads and anchor-free prediction mechanisms \cite{terven2023}.

YOLOv8, released in 2023 as a commercial implementation by Ultralytics \cite{ultralytics}, introduced architectural innovations addressing limitations of earlier single-stage detectors.
The anchor-free detection strategy employs dual detection heads that independently identify object presence and predict bounding box coordinates, simplifying transfer learning and enhancing adaptability to varying aspect ratios.
% The backbone incorporates Cross-Stage Partial Fusion (C2f) modules that enhance feature reuse while reducing computational overhead compared to earlier designs.
Modern loss functions, including \ac{CIoU} and Distribution Focal Loss, improve localization precision and boundary regression stability~\cite{redmon2016}.
Max pooling and image down-sampling also contribute to CNN speed, but impose a fundamental constraint on \ac{SOD}, where objects smaller than approximately 16-32 pixels produce low-confidence or unstable detections~\cite{rekavandi2022}.
% However, the multi-scale detection architecture with feature-map strides of 8, 16, and 32 pixels imposes fundamental constraints: objects smaller than approximately 16-32 pixels produce low-confidence or unstable detections due to progressive downsampling~\cite{rekavandi2022}.
For maritime applications, down-sampling imagery to 640×640 pixel inputs corresponds to smaller or distant targets near the horizon occupying less than 0.5-1\% of the image frame.
These architectural properties make YOLOv8 suitable for real-time use; however, the model's constraints on \ac{SOD}, along with other environmental factors, motivate the investigation of complementary sensing modalities.

\section{LiDAR-Based Object Detection for Autonomous Systems}

\subsection{Deep Learning Approaches for Point Cloud Processing}

\Ac{LiDAR} sensors provide three-dimensional geometric measurements of nearby spatial structure by measuring the time-of-flight of laser pulses.
By recording the delay between pulse emission and return, \ac{LiDAR} systems compute precise distances to surfaces within their field of view. As the sensor scans its environment, these distance measurements are combined with its orientation and position to generate a dense set of spatial coordinates, which are saved as point clouds.
Depending on the scanning mechanism, \ac{LiDAR} systems may produce either structured samples arranged in a regular, repeating pattern or unstructured samples with irregular spatial distributions. This distinction strongly influences the types of processing strategies that can be applied.

Structured LiDARs, such as rotating multi-beam sensors with fixed vertical channels, generate data that can be reorganized into a two-dimensional “range image” where rows correspond to laser channels, and columns correspond to azimuth samples. This rasterization creates a matrix, often 64 $\times$ 2048, which is analogous to an image on which \acp{CNN} can be applied. 
Range-image methods like SqueezeSeg~\cite{wu2018} and RangeNet++~\cite{milioto2019} use two-dimensional \acp{CNN} for point-wise semantic segmentation. In these pipelines, object-level detections are obtained by clustering the segmented points using methods such as Euclidean cluster extraction~\cite{rusu2011} or DBSCAN~\cite{Ester1996}.

In contrast, unstructured LiDARs do not produce measurements that align with a stable grid. Their point clouds exhibit non-uniform sampling, which lacks a consistent angular pattern and prevents rasterization into a range image. 
Processing this type of data requires algorithms that operate directly on irregular three-dimensional samples~\cite{zhou2018, shi2019}.

Deep learning has emerged as the dominant offline approach for point cloud object detection, with neural networks designed to learn hierarchical feature representations from three-dimensional data. These learned approaches leverage large labeled datasets to extract spatial patterns and semantic information directly from point coordinates. The following architectural families represent the evolution of neural network-based methods, progressing from point-based processing to voxelization strategies and hybrid architectures.

Point-based networks (PointNet~\cite{qi2017}) operate on raw point coordinates but scale unfavorably with point cloud size~\cite{shi2019}. Voxelization methods address scalability by discretizing point clouds into regular grids. Zhou and Tuzel~\cite{zhou2018} developed VoxelNet, which transforms sparse point clouds into dense volumetric representations. Yan et al.\cite{yan2018} extended this approach with SECOND, introducing sparse convolutional operations that improve efficiency by processing only occupied voxels. Lang et al.\cite{lang2019} proposed PointPillars, which encodes point clouds into vertical columns to enable efficient 2D convolutional processing. The fundamental trade-off in voxelization is quantization error: small objects or fine geometric details may be lost during discretization~\cite{zhou2018}. Hybrid architectures such as PV-RCNN~\cite{shi2021} combine the strengths of point- and voxel-based processing and achieve state-of-the-art accuracy for automotive datasets, but they assume dense, ground-supported returns that do not hold in maritime environments where water surfaces yield sparse reflections~\cite{kunz2005}.

Recent innovations demonstrate continued advancement, but with substantial computational requirements.
BEVFusion~\cite{liang2022, liu2023b} achieves state-of-the-art accuracy but requires desktop-class GPUs with substantial power consumption and achieves only 15-20 frames per second~\cite{liu2023b}—this power budget alone exceeds total available power for many small autonomous vessels.
Transformer-based architectures that apply self-attention to point clouds offer theoretical advantages but require multi-GPU configurations with substantial power consumption and have not been validated under maritime-specific conditions~\cite{xie2024}.

Beyond computational constraints, these neural network-based approaches face limitations for safety-critical maritime applications.
Enforcing strict rules in neural networks, such as ensuring object separation distances, is difficult due to their non-deterministic nature.
This limitation is particularly problematic when using spatial data for navigational purposes and obstacle avoidance~\cite{coyleE}.
Given this persistent challenge, there is a continued need to investigate deterministic approaches that can provide stricter guarantees for safety-critical applications. 

\subsection{Deterministic Geometric Alternatives}

In contrast to neural network-based approaches, deterministic geometric algorithms offer advantages for resource-constrained embedded platforms, where runtime predictability and minimal training data requirements are highly valued.
Coyle~\cite{coyleE} developed \ac{GB-CACHE}, a deterministic approach specifically designed for uncrewed systems perceiving objects on flat surfaces, including water.
GB-CACHE efficiently segments point clouds through grid-based spatial partitioning, then extracts two-dimensional concave hull boundaries that accurately represent objects with irregular geometries.
Computational efficiency analysis demonstrates real-time feasibility on low to mid-grade computing platforms~\cite{coyleE}.

The deterministic nature of GB-CACHE provides specific operational advantages.
Processing latency remains bounded and predictable, eliminating variable inference times characteristic of neural networks, where batch size and scene complexity affect throughput. 
Two such advantages are beneficial for navigation. 
First, the extracted concave hull is also more valuable for navigation than convex two or three-dimensional bounding boxes, commonly produced by neural network methods.
Second, its deterministic formulation allows strict rule enforcement on spatial constraints, which is essential for obstacle avoidance, whereas neural approaches provide probabilistic outputs without strict guarantees~\cite{coyleE}.
Additionally, training data requirements are minimal. The algorithm requires only geometric parameters rather than extensive labeled datasets—a practical advantage for maritime deployment where training data scarcity persists.

\subsection{Maritime-Specific LiDAR Challenges}

Maritime \ac{LiDAR} performance differs fundamentally from automotive scenarios.
Automotive systems achieve near 100\% return rates on asphalt, providing dense point clouds with abundant ground plane context~\cite{roriz2022}.
Water surfaces produce specular reflection, yielding only 10\% return rates versus near-100\% on asphalt~\cite{kunz2005, roriz2022}, creating sparse point clouds lacking dense ground plane context exploited by automotive algorithms~\cite{zhou2018, shi2019}.
Kunz et al.~\cite{kunz2005} measured return rates over calm water as low as 10\% of the emitted pulses, noting that water surface characteristics produce significantly weaker returns than small floating targets, creating a favorable contrast for target detection despite a sparse environmental context.

The sparsity of water returns poses challenges to detection algorithms designed for dense point clouds in automotive applications.
Maritime applications lack dense reference, necessitating alternative spatial reasoning strategies when objects appear isolated without contextual grounding.
Atmospheric effects reduce effective range by 50-70\% in heavy haze~\cite{roriz2022}, while wave-induced motion requires IMU-based motion compensation, introducing processing latency~\cite{xie2024, ahmed2024}.
Platform motion caused by continuous wave-induced pitch, roll, and heave motions vary dynamically with sea state.
Ahmed et al.~\cite{ahmed2024} developed specialized Kalman filtering approaches tailored for maritime platform motion, reporting 25-30\% improvement in detection accuracy when compensating for tilt-induced point cloud distortions.

While several recent studies attempt to address these maritime-specific challenges, significant validation gaps remain.
Xie et al.~\cite{xie2024} achieved an overall detection accuracy of 74.1\% for ship detection within the range of five to 40 meters using LiDAR-based detection coupled with multiple neural networks.
Detection methods originally designed for dense ground-plane returns require fundamental adaptation for sparse maritime point clouds where objects lack grounding context~\cite{kunz2005}.
A systematic evaluation of detection performance versus water-surface characteristics, motion-compensation quality, and embedded-platform constraints (including latency, power, and throughput) is absent from the reviewed literature~\cite{xie2024, ahmed2024}.

The complementary limitations of camera and \ac{LiDAR} modalities—vision failing under extreme lighting while LiDAR struggles with sparse returns—suggest potential benefits from sensor fusion.

\section{Sensor Fusion Paradigms}

Sensor fusion combines information from multiple modalities to overcome the limitations of individual sensors.
Recent surveys~\cite{feng2021, tufekci2023, huang2024} identify fusion level—early, mid, or late—as the primary architectural distinction determining computational requirements, calibration sensitivity, and failure mode propagation relevant to maritime deployment.
Each fusion paradigm offers distinct trade-offs between peak accuracy potential, robustness to sensor degradation, and real-time implementation feasibility~\cite{feng2021}.

\subsection{Early Fusion: Data-Level Integration}

Early fusion combines raw sensor data before feature extraction, projecting measurements from different modalities into a common representation~\cite{cui2022}.
Cui et al.~\cite{cui2022} note that early fusion enables networks to learn joint features during training.
PointPainting exemplifies this paradigm by projecting \ac{LiDAR} points into camera image coordinates, assigning semantic labels from segmentation networks, then processing the semantically enriched point cloud through object detectors~\cite{cui2022}.

However, early fusion imposes stringent calibration and synchronization requirements, creating practical deployment challenges.
Projection errors of even a few degrees of rotation or centimeters of translation misalign features, corrupting the joint representation~\cite{cui2022}.
Temporal synchronization must be precise because any error in timing creates spatial inconsistencies and reduces fusion accuracy. Sensor noise and failures also pass directly into the fused result. Corrupted camera images caused by lens occlusion or extreme lighting produce inaccurate semantic labels, and these errors affect every projected LiDAR point~\cite{cui2022}.
For maritime platforms experiencing continuous wave-induced motion and intermittent sun glare, calibration sensitivity and failure propagation of early fusion represent significant operational risks.
Maritime platform dynamics introduce additional calibration complexity beyond automotive scenarios.
While automotive research established general calibration tolerances~\cite{cui2022}, wave-induced motion can create time-varying calibration errors even when initial alignment is precise.
% These dynamic calibration challenges affect all fusion paradigms but with varying severity depending on the required precision of spatial correspondence.

\subsection{Mid Fusion: Feature-Level Integration}

Mid fusion extracts features independently from each modality before combining them in intermediate network layers~\cite{huang2024}.
Huang et al. systematically compare fusion paradigms, finding that mid fusion balances accuracy and robustness. independent feature extraction prevents raw sensor corruption from propagating between modalities, yet learned fusion layers capture cross-modal correlations unavailable to late fusion ~\cite{huang2024}.
It is important to note, however, that mid-fusion architectures generally require both modalities to produce learned feature embeddings. As a result, mid fusion is typically applicable only when both sensing pipelines are neural-network-based, and does not extend naturally to systems that combine a deterministic method with a neural model~\cite{baltruvsaitis2018}.

BEVFusion~\cite{liang2022, liu2023b} transforms camera features into bird's-eye-view representations compatible with \ac{LiDAR} BEV features, combining them through efficient convolution operations.
Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023b} note that BEVFusion achieves state-of-the-art performance but requires desktop-class GPU hardware, exceeding embedded platform capabilities by an order of magnitude.
For battery-powered maritime platforms with constrained power budgets, mid-fusion's computational demands preclude direct deployment without substantial architectural optimization.

\subsection{Late Fusion: Decision-Level Integration}

Late fusion operates on independent object detections from each modality, combining outputs through spatial association~\cite{wang2020a, pang2020}.
This approach maintains modular detection pipelines, fusing results at the decision level after individual sensor processing is complete.
While sacrificing joint feature learning enabled by earlier fusion, late fusion offers architectural properties advantageous for resource-constrained maritime deployments.

Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020a} survey multi-sensor fusion in automated driving, emphasizing that late fusion enables graceful degradation, where sensor failures affect only individual detection streams without corrupting fusion outputs.
Pang et al.~\cite{pang2020} developed Camera-LiDAR Object Candidates (CLOCs), which fuse detection candidates before non-maximum suppression, training networks to predict fused detection quality based on geometric and semantic consistency between modalities.

Late fusion's modular architecture provides several practical advantages for maritime deployment.
Computational requirements scale approximately linearly with sensor count rather than exponentially as with joint processing~\cite{wang2020a}.
Processing latency remains predictable because each modality follows fixed pipelines without runtime cross-modal dependencies during feature extraction.
Adding or removing sensors requires minimal system redesign—new detection streams integrate through updated fusion modules without modifying existing pipelines~\cite{feng2021}.
Most critically for maritime applications, individual sensor failures degrade performance gracefully rather than causing catastrophic fusion failure~\cite{huang2024}.
Temporal synchronization requirements are relaxed compared to early fusion: late fusion spatial association operates on object-level regions rather than pixel-level correspondences, tolerating larger timing offsets—typically 50 to 100 milliseconds versus 10 milliseconds for early fusion~\cite{fung2017}.
For maritime platforms where precise hardware synchronization may be impractical and wave-induced motion creates apparent object movement, relaxed timing tolerances represent operational advantages.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis and Maritime Fusion Gaps}

Huang et al.~\cite{huang2024} systematically evaluate fusion paradigms under simulated sensor degradation and synchronization error.
Late fusion maintains superior robustness when individual sensors fail or produce degraded outputs through graceful degradation.
However, under ideal conditions with clean data and perfect calibration, mid fusion achieves higher peak accuracy by learning cross-modal correlations unavailable to late fusion~\cite{huang2024}.
Wang et al.~\cite{wang2020a} note that mid-fusion architectures approximately double processing requirements compared to single-modality detection.
For battery-powered maritime platforms, increased power consumption directly reduces mission endurance.

These findings from automotive research suggest that operational requirements and constraints should inform the selection of fusion strategy.
Early and mid fusion optimize for peak accuracy under ideal conditions but require precise calibration, tight synchronization, and substantial computational resources~\cite{feng2021, cui2022}.
Late fusion prioritizes robustness and computational efficiency under degraded conditions, accepting reduced peak accuracy compared to deeper fusion strategies~\cite{huang2024, wang2020a}.

Automotive fusion methods~\cite{feng2021, cui2022, huang2024} evaluate performance on terrestrial datasets where environmental characteristics differ fundamentally from maritime scenarios—water reflections, extreme dynamic range, sparse \ac{LiDAR} returns, and platform motion require empirical validation.
Computational requirements, power consumption, and latency characteristics on representative embedded hardware are rarely reported~\cite{liu2023a}.
Systematic evaluation sweeping timing offsets to establish acceptable synchronization tolerances for maritime platforms is absent~\cite{huang2024}, and wave-induced motion creates time-varying calibration errors differing from static errors typical of ground vehicles~\cite{cui2022}.
Most fusion studies report performance without rigorous comparison against optimized single-modality baselines~\cite{farahnakian2020, haghbayan2018, clunie2021}.

\section{Temporal and Spatial Alignment in Fusion Systems}

Accurate fusion depends on establishing spatial and temporal correspondence between sensors.
Spatial calibration defines rigid-body transformation relating sensor coordinate frames, while temporal synchronization ensures observations represent the exact moment in time.
Precision requirements vary with the fusion paradigm—early fusion demands tighter tolerances than late fusion due to the need for pixel-level data alignment.

Extrinsic calibration establishes a geometric relationship between sensors, typically represented as a rotation matrix and a translation vector.
Traditional calibration employs fiducial targets, such as checkerboards or AprilTags, visible to both sensors, solving for the transformation through least-squares optimization.
Manual calibration remains standard for many research systems, as it provides the precision necessary for reliable geometric correspondence.
Automated calibration methods have emerged as alternatives. Iyer et al.~\cite{iyer2018} developed CalibNet, which uses deep learning to predict calibration parameters directly from sensor data.
Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2024} proposed CalibFormer, a transformer-based automatic LiDAR-camera calibration network~\cite{schneider2017, wu2021}.
However, these automated approaches require sufficient scene structure and may fail in textureless maritime environments, which are often dominated by sky and water.
Calibration accuracy directly influences fusion quality. Small pose errors can produce large spatial misalignments, and rotation errors above one degree or translation errors above ten millimeters are known to degrade feature-level and early-fusion performance. Prior work shows that these tolerances tighten as sensor baselines increase ~\cite{geiger2012,huang2024,park2014extrinsic,bijelic2020robust}

Temporal synchronization ensures observations represent the same world state~\cite{westenberger2011}.
Timing offsets create spatial misalignment: a vessel moving at 5 meters per second experiences 0.5-meter displacement in 100 milliseconds.
IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol achieves sub-microsecond accuracy~\cite{ptp2008}, while Network Time Protocol provides millisecond-level accuracy~\cite{furgale2013, liu2021}.
Early fusion requires synchronization within 10 milliseconds; late fusion tolerates 50-100 milliseconds~\cite{huang2024}.
Maritime wave-induced motion produces apparent object movement requiring motion compensation beyond timestamp alignment.

\section{Research Gaps and Dissertation Motivation}

Despite substantial progress in multimodal perception for autonomous systems, several critical gaps remain for maritime applications.
These gaps span algorithmic approaches, computational implementation, and empirical validation resources.

Most published fusion research emphasizes early or mid-level integration, with limited evaluation of decision-level approaches under maritime conditions.
Comparative studies in automotive contexts demonstrate that late fusion offers superior robustness under sensor dropout or environmental degradation—conditions frequently encountered during maritime operations~\cite{huang2024, wang2020a}.
However, these findings primarily derive from terrestrial datasets where environmental characteristics and sensor failure modes differ substantially from those in maritime scenarios.
Whether late fusion maintains similar robustness advantages under maritime-specific challenges, including water reflections, HDR lighting extremes, and platform motion, remains an open empirical question in the reviewed literature.
% This dissertation addresses this gap through systematic evaluation of late fusion performance under maritime conditions, as described in Chapter~\ref{realtime_object_detection}.

Real-time fusion implementation on embedded maritime hardware remains underexplored.
Early and mid-fusion methods often exceed the computational budgets of embedded platforms, requiring desktop-class GPUs with typical power consumption of 200-400 watts for real-time operation.
Late fusion's reduced computational requirements suggest potential feasibility for small autonomous systems.
However, systematic evaluation of accuracy-latency-power trade-offs on desktop-class maritime hardware is lacking in prior maritime fusion research.
This dissertation evaluates YOLO using Minions's onboard main PC (NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 GPU). Three pre-trained model variants (nano, small, medium) are compared, establishing baseline performance and selecting the small-sized variant over the medium model for its equivalent accuracy and inference speed, as detailed in Chapter~\ref{realtime_object_detection}.
Power consumption is particularly critical for battery-operated platforms where computing loads directly affect mission endurance.

Few labeled multimodal maritime datasets exist with synchronized \ac{LiDAR} and camera observations under diverse conditions \cite{huang2025} (see Tables~\ref{tbl:agv_datasets}-\ref{tbl:usv_datasets}).
Existing datasets are either single-modality, which limits fusion research, lack precise synchronization, preventing temporal analysis, or provide limited weather and lighting diversity, thereby restricting generalization assessment.
This data gap directly impedes algorithm development and validation.
For this research, a synchronized \ac{LiDAR}-camera dataset was collected aboard the \acl{WAM-V} platform during operational deployments, providing 9,790,476 sensor messages and approximately 49,898 individual HDR images as described in Section~\ref{sec:sensor_data_dataset}.

This dissertation develops and validates a late-fusion framework for real-time \ac{LiDAR}-camera perception on embedded maritime platforms.
Sensor data is fused in the camera's image frame, and this choice of reference frame is discussed in Section~\ref{fusion_ref_frame}.
The approach is motivated by the advantages of late fusion for maritime deployment, including a modular architecture that isolates sensor failures, computational efficiency compatible with embedded hardware constraints, and robustness to calibration and synchronization errors compared to tightly coupled fusion approaches.
Research methodology quantitatively evaluates accuracy, timing, and computational efficiency through controlled experiments and real-world validation aboard the Minion research platform.

The following chapter describes experimental methodology employed to address these research questions, including detailed hardware configuration, sensor calibration procedures, data collection protocols, and late fusion algorithm design.

\subsubsection{Image Resolution}
\label{image_resolution}
For a fixed FOV, camera resolution determines which targets can be resolved at a given sensing distance. 
Ultimately, adequate perception across the operational envelope requires resolving the smallest object at the maximum required detection range. 
The relationship between an object's physical width $\mathit{w}_{obj}$ and pixel width within an image $\mathit{w}_{\text{px}}$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pixel_width}
\mathit{w}_{\text{px}} = f \; \frac{N_x}{S_w}\frac{\mathit{w}_{obj}}{d},
\end{equation}

where $d$ is the physical width of the object, $f$ is the combined focal length of the camera and lens \acl{EFL}, $N_x$ is the horizontal image resolution in pixels, and $S_w$ is the width of the image sensor.
This equation can be used to determine camera requirements if the true size of the objects is known, or to determine a reasonable expectation of object resolution if the camera values are known.
This relationship is also critical for determining the distortion in each camera/lens system and is further detailed in section~\ref{camera_intrinsics}.

Defining the minimum resolution requirement for an object in the image frame requires an understanding of the object detection method used with visual cameras (discussed in Section~\ref{yolo}), as well as the objects being detected (discussed in Section~\ref{sec:sensor_data_dataset}). 
The image detection architecture is discussed in section ~\ref{yolo}, but it is sufficient to know that this method scales each input image to $640 \times 640$ pixels for processing efficiency.
This should be considered when defining the minimum pixel density of a camera's sensor, as it alters (\ref{eq:pixel_width}), shrinking the pixel width of the object from  $\mathit{w}_{\text{px}}$ to $\mathit{w}'_{\text{px}}$ when processed. $\mathit{w}'_{\text{px}}$ is defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pixel_width2}
\mathit{w}'_{\text{px}} = f \; \frac{640 \text{px}}{S_w}\frac{\mathit{w}_{obj}}{d}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Dynamic Range}
\label{dynamic_range}

Dynamic range describes the ratio between the brightest and darkest signal levels a camera sensor can record.
Light levels that exceed the camera sensor's range cause the image to appear white or ``blown-out'', while light levels that are too low will appear darker, with detail being indistinguishable from noise.
\acp{USV} and \acp{AGS} routinely operate in environments where bright sky reflections and deep shadows are common, often within the same scene.
The luminance range present in a typical outdoor scene under normal daylight conditions is on the order of $\sim\!120\,\text{dB}$ \cite{Onzon2021}.
Therefore, selecting a camera with insufficient dynamic range may lead to washed-out signals in direct sunlight or lost detail in shadows.

Dynamic range, expressed in dB, is given by
\begin{equation}
 \text{Dynamic Range (dB)} = 20 \log{\left( \frac{N_{sat}}{N_{noise}}\right) }
\end{equation}
where $N_{sat}/N_{noise}$ is the ratio of the saturation to the minimum signal a camera sensor can detect above background noise.
\ac{HDR} imaging is commonly achieved with one of two methods. 
The first is sequential multi-exposure \ac{HDR}, in which multiple frames captured at different exposure settings are combined to extend the tonal range \cite{Reinhard2010}. An example is provided in Figure \ref{fig:hdr_example}.
While effective for static scenes, as the relative velocity between the sensor and objects within the frame increases, ghosting and motion blur can occur.
The second method uses an in-sensor technique called \ac{DCG}, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:DCG_example}.
This method assigns multiple exposure durations to different regions of the imaging sensor to achieve results similar to multi-exposure HDR within a single captured frame.
This method avoids ghosting, but collects fewer total photons than the multi-exposure method.

\begin{figure}[hp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Images/ghosting.png}
    \caption{Five sequential image frames captured with separate exposure settings (left). These frames are digitally combined to achieve a higher dynamic range (right), but may result in ghosting in the final composite image (blue box). \cite{Reinhard2010}.}
    \label{fig:hdr_example}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[hp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\linewidth]{Images/dcg.png}
    \caption{Visualization of dual-conversion gain: separate regions of the same imaging sensor are set to capture multiple levels of light exposure during a single capture \cite{koifman2013}.}
    \label{fig:DCG_example}
\end{figure}

The IMX490 CMOS sensor in the Leopard Imaging camera achieves 120 dB of dynamic range via Sony’s \ac{DOL-HDR} architecture. 
This approach blends software and hardware approaches to effectively bridge the gap between multi-exposure and DCG methods.
The CMOS sensor is covered with large and small photodiode micro-lenses (see Figure~\ref{fig:HDR_CMOS}) so that each sub-pixel size experiences a different photon flux during a given exposure time. Additionally, each sub-pixel output is read at both high and low ``conversion gains'' to effectively read multiple exposure levels within a single image capture.
This method offers a robust solution for high-contrast, high-motion imaging scenes, simultaneously eliminating motion artifacts from multi-exposure \ac{HDR} and enabling precise temporal alignment for sensor fusion.

\subsection{PinPoint GPS/INS} \label{sensors_GPS}

Reliable global localization and precise timing are fundamental requirements for maritime autonomy. Regulatory guidance from the International Maritime Organization (IMO), including the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs) \cite{COLREGs1972} and the IMO Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS) Regulatory Scoping Exercise (MSC.1/Circ.1638, 2021) \cite{IMO_MASS_RSE_2021}, establishes that autonomous vessels must maintain situational awareness, safe navigation, and position information equivalent to conventional crewed vessels. 
These expectations require continuous knowledge of a vessel’s position, heading, and motion state.

These same capabilities are essential for the sensing and perception tasks used in this research. 
Aggregating LiDAR measurements over time requires each scan to be referenced to a stable inertial frame. 
The platform’s position and orientation must be known at each measurement so that point clouds can be aligned in the presence of wave-induced motion disturbances.
Precise timing also plays an equally important role in this calibration, and a GPS-disciplined clock provides the synchronized timestamps needed to align LiDAR, camera, and platform state data so that each modality corresponds to the same physical instant.

The Minion \ac{USV} employs a Torc Robotics PinPoint \ac{GPS}/\ac{INS} unit to satisfy these requirements. 
The system integrates a differential-corrected \ac{GPS} receiver, a multi-axis \ac{IMU}, and a dual-antenna configuration that produces precise position, velocity, and orientation estimates. 
Position accuracy is further improved through a subscription-based correction service that supplies real-time differential updates to the GPS solution.
The GPS receiver operates at 5 Hz, and the IMU provides inertial measurements at 105.4 Hz. 
The GPS receiver also disciplines the internal oscillator to maintain timing accuracy on the order of tens of nanoseconds. 
This level of accuracy enables the synchronized operation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:calibration_temporal} and supports the consistent aggregation of LiDAR measurements for real-time perception and data fusion.

\section{Spatial Calibration} \label{sec:calibration_spatial}

\subsection{Extrinsic Calibration}

Minion’s multi-sensor perception suite integrates several complementary sensing modalities, each operating in its own coordinate frame and maintaining its own internal clock. To combine measurements from the cameras, LiDAR units, and GPS/INS, the spatial relationships between their frames of reference must be established, and the timing of their data streams must be aligned.

Because all sensors are rigidly mounted to the vehicle, the geometric relationship between any two sensor frames remains constant. 
This relationship is represented by a static transform, a fixed rigid-body mapping consisting of a rotation matrix and a translation vector.
Each sensor pair has its own static transform which remains valid so long as the physical relationship between them does not change. 

Extrinsic calibration determines these static transforms by solving for the rigid-body relationship between two coordinate frames. The transformation from reference frame $\{A\}$ to frame $\{B\}$ is represented as ${}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{T}$ and back to $\{A\}$ as ${}_{B}^{A}\mathbf{T}$ where

\begin{align}
{}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{T} &=
\begin{bmatrix}
{}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{R} & {}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{t} \\
0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}, \\[1ex]
{}_{B}^{A}\mathbf{T} =
\left({}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{T}\right)^{-1}
&=
\begin{bmatrix}
\left({}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{R}\right)^{\top} &
-\left({}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{R}\right)^{\top} {}_A^{B}\mathbf{t} \\
0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}

and $_{A}^{B}\mathbf{R} \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is the rotation matrix describing the orientation of frame $\{A\}$ relative to frame $\{B\}$, and $_{A}^{B}\mathbf{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is the translation vector locating the origin of frame $\{A\}$ with respect to frame $\{B\}$.  
A point $^{A}\mathbf{p}$ expressed in frame $\{A\}$ can therefore be transformed into frame $\{B\}$ as

\begin{equation}
    ^{B}\mathbf{p} =
    {}_{A}^{B}\mathbf{R} \,{}^{A}\mathbf{p} + _{A}^{B}\mathbf{t}.
\label{eq:transform}
\end{equation}

These transforms can be composed to move through multiple reference frames in sequence. For example, a point expressed in frame $\{A\}$ can be mapped to frame $\{C\}$ through frame $\{B\}$ by multiplying the corresponding transforms as ${}_{B}^{C}\mathbf{T} {}_{B}^{A}\mathbf{T}$. This provides a direct and efficient way to express measurements collected in one reference frame within any other frame defined by the system.
Once estimated, these extrinsic parameters are stored within Minion's software, where ROS links the reference frames  as represented by the transform tree in Figure~\ref{fig:tf_tree}.

The resulting hierarchy, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tf_tree}, defines how each sensor relates to the global \texttt{map} frame and how data can be moved between coordinate systems. Any physical change to a sensor or its mounting geometry requires recalibration of the transforms associated with that sensor, because errors at any node propagate through all downstream frames.

At the top of the transform tree is the map frame, and is the only reference frame that is not at any sensor's origin. The transformation between the map and the GPS sensor is time dependent, updating Minion's position and orientation within the map frame as it moves, and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:calibration_temporal}.
% As Minion moves, its position and orientation change relative to the map, and accurate pose information ensures that objects remain correctly represented in the destination frame.

The spatial calibration process begins by establishing the static transforms between the Pinpoint GPS and the central Livox unit. Centralizing these relationships around the center LiDAR frame minimizes accumulated error along frame chains and provides a consistent reference for all other sensors.
Next, the port and starboard Livox reference frames are aligned to the center, ensuring the three units produce a cohesive, unified point cloud.
This is followed by calibration of the camera intrinsics, which determines the camera's focal length and distortion characteristics, allowing three-dimensional points in the camera's reference frame to be accurately positioned within the two-dimensional image plane.
Finally, the extrinsic between the camera and the central Livox is obtained to accurately position the LiDAR data in the camera's frame of reference.
Any physical modification to the hardware, such as repositioning or replacing a sensor, requires that the modified sensor be re-calibrated.

Although a prior calibration workflow for an earlier configuration of this sensor suite was conducted by Thompson \cite{thompson2023}, the camera enclosure has undergone significant maintenance, invalidating that calibration.
The mounts for each Livox Horizon were upgraded from wooden templates to more durable stainless steel, and the central Livox was replaced due to a hardware failure.
Because accurate sensor fusion depends on calibration parameters matching the exact hardware and mechanical configuration used during data collection, a complete spatial recalibration was required.
The following sections will describe the method used to calibrate these transforms and the results obtained.

\subsubsection{GPS to LiDAR}

The goal of GPS–LiDAR spatial calibration is to estimate the fixed rigid transform ${}_{G}^{L}\mathbf{T}$ that maps the GPS/INS frame $\{G\}$ into the LiDAR frame $\{L\}$. This transform includes a constant rotation ${}_{G}^{L}\mathbf{R}$ and translation ${}_{G}^{L}\mathbf{t}$ that describe the mounting geometry between the two sensors. A standard approach is to record a static environment from multiple platform poses while simultaneously collecting GPS-derived poses and LiDAR point clouds. For each pair of poses, the GPS/INS provides the relative platform motion in its own frame,
${}_{G_i}^{G_j}\mathbf{T}$,
and the corresponding LiDAR scans are aligned using a registration method such as \ac{ICP} to obtain the relative motion in the LiDAR frame,
${}_{L_i}^{L_j}\mathbf{T}$.

Each matched motion pair satisfies the hand–eye relationship
\begin{equation}
    {}_{G_i}^{G_j}\mathbf{T} {}_{G}^{L}\mathbf{T}
    =
    {}_{G}^{L}\mathbf{T} {}_{L_i}^{L_j}\mathbf{T},
\end{equation}
which states that the GPS-observed motion and LiDAR-observed motion are consistent under the unknown extrinsic transform. Expanding this relationship into rotational and translational components produces two linear systems. The rotation ${}_{G}^{L}\mathbf{R}$ is obtained by solving a homogeneous linear system constructed from all motion pairs, followed by projection onto the space of valid rotation matrices. Once the rotation is known, the translation ${}_{G}^{L}\mathbf{t}$ is estimated using a linear least-squares fit based on the relative motions in both frames.

\subsection{Camera Intrinsic Calibration} \label{camera_intrinsics}

Camera intrinsic parameters describe the internal geometry of the camera and define how three-dimensional points are projected onto the two-dimensional image plane.  
These parameters describe the path light takes through the lens and determine how real-world positions correspond to image pixel coordinates.  
The intrinsic model is typically divided into an idealized pinhole projection and a lens distortion model.

Under the pinhole model, the projection of a point onto the image plane is given by the intrinsic matrix:
\begin{equation} \label{K_matrix}
\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
\mathbf{K}
\begin{bmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{K} =
\begin{bmatrix}
f_x & s & c_x \\
0   & f_y & c_y \\
0   & 0   & 1
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

% \begin{equation}
%     \mathbf{K} = 
%     \begin{bmatrix}
%         f_x & s & c_x \\
%         0 & f_y & c_y \\
%         0 & 0 & 1
%     \end{bmatrix},
% \end{equation}

where $(f_x, f_y)$ are focal lengths in pixels, $(c_x, c_y)$ denotes the principal point, $s$ represents the skew factor (generally $s=0$ for modern sensors), and the camera frame coordinates shown given as ($x'$, $y'$), and pixel coordinates as $(u,v)$.

Real lenses introduce distortion, primarily radial, due to refraction near the outer edge.  
This effect, most pronounced in wide-angle optics, causes straight lines to appear curved.  
Radial distortion is modeled using polynomial coefficients $k_1$, $k_2$, and $k_3$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        x' &= x_n(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6), \\
        y' &= y_n(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4 + k_3 r^6),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}


where $(x', y')$ are distorted coordinates, $(x_n, y_n)$ are normalized coordinates, and $r = \sqrt{x_n^2 + y_n^2}$ is the radial distance from the optical center.  
Although this model omits asymmetric distortions, high-quality lenses typically minimize such effects.  
Even with manufacturer-provided parameters for cameras and lenses, recalibration ensures sub-pixel accuracy when aligning \ac{LiDAR} projections with image data.

To estimate intrinsic parameters, images of a planar checkerboard with known square dimensions are captured from multiple orientations and distances.  
Its grid of high-contrast corners serves as stable reference points for gradient-based corner detection algorithms such as the Harris method \cite{derpanis}.  
Calibration software uses these detected points to compute statistically optimal parameters across the whole image set.

Effective calibration requires dozens of images taken at diverse depths and orientations spanning the camera’s operational field of view.  
Checkerboard dimensions should approximate the scale of observed objects to ensure that the chosen camera provides adequate spatial resolution (as discussed in Section~\ref{image_resolution}).  
A total of thirty images were captured, with the checkerboard placed in distinct locations within the camera. Each image included 40 calibration points, yielding a total of 930 reference points across all images.

\subsection{LiDAR to Camera Extrinsic Calibration} \label{camLidar_calib}

Camera–LiDAR extrinsic calibration begins by placing the planar checkerboard target within the overlapping field of view of both sensors.  
Corresponding points on the target are observed in the \texttt{livox\_center} frame $\{L\}$ as ${}^{L}\mathbf{p} = [{}^LX,\,{}^LY,\,{}^L Z]^T$ and in the camera image as pixel coordinates $(u, v)$.  
% These associations provide the basis for estimating the rigid-body transform between the LiDAR frame $\{L\}$ and the camera frame $\{C\}$.

A LiDAR point ${}^{L}\mathbf{P}$ is transformed into the camera frame $\{C\}$ using Equation~\ref{eq:transform},
where ${}^{C}_{L}\mathbf{R}$ is the rotation taking coordinates from frame $\{L\}$ to frame $\{C\}$, and  
${}^{L}\mathbf{p}_{C\,\mathrm{ORIG}}$ is the vector from the camera origin to the LiDAR origin expressed in frame $\{L\}$.

Once expressed in the camera frame as ${}^{C}\mathbf{p} = [X_C, Y_C, Z_C]^T$, the point is mapped into normalized image coordinates through
\begin{equation}
    x_n = \frac{X_C}{Z_C}, \qquad y_n = \frac{Y_C}{Z_C}.
\end{equation}

Normalization represents the projection of the 3D point onto the unit focal plane of an ideal pinhole camera, separating geometric perspective from camera-specific parameters.

Radial distortion is then applied to obtain distorted normalized coordinates $(x', y')$:
\begin{equation}
    r^2 = x_n^2 + y_n^2, 
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    x' = x_n \left(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4\right),\quad
    y' = y_n \left(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4\right).
\end{equation}

Radial distortion thus produces the distorted normalized coordinates (x', y'), which are later mapped into pixel space using the camera intrinsic matrix $\mathbf{K}$ in Equation~\ref{K_matrix}.

\subsubsection{Initial Calibration}
An initial LiDAR to camera transform is obtained using MATLAB’s Lidar Camera Calibration tool. This tool detects the checkerboard pose in each image, fits a plane to the corresponding LiDAR returns, and solves a nonlinear least squares optimization that aligns the image derived board pose with the LiDAR derived plane \cite{matlab_calibration}. This produces a preliminary extrinsic transform that serves as the starting point for later refinement.

The calibration procedure requires a static point cloud that corresponds to an image of the same scene. 
Data is collected using a method similar to that required by the MATLAB Camera Calibration tool, where the checkerboard calibration target is placed around the stationary cameras' \ac{FOV}, but with both the camera and LiDAR capturing data simultaneously. 
To ensure can capture a dense point cloud, the checkerboard is held stationary for five seconds at each location. 

After data collection, the high dynamic range video and the LiDAR messages are loaded into MATLAB and manually aligned to the periods where the checkerboard remains static. 
For each identified image frame, a window of 0.5 seconds of LiDAR returns was centered on that timestamp, over which the LiDAR returns are concatenated.
This process is repeated for each checkerboard position to produce a matched set of image frames and dense point clouds.
To reduce the likelihood that the calibration algorithm selects an incorrect planar region, each point cloud is reduced to a small three-dimensional \ac{ROI} that isolates the calibration target as the largest planar object within the reduced point cloud. The resulting pairs of isolated LiDAR returns and checkerboard images are then processed by the MATLAB tool to estimate the initial transform.

Despite this capability, the Jetson Xavier cannot support the complete Minion autonomy stack, and currently lacks the minimum OS requirement to run \ac{ROS}. 
Instead, it functions as a dedicated edge-processing node for processing camera compression and network streaming. 
Raw video from each camera is received over USB3.0 and routed through a multi-branch GStreamer pipeline.
This process is described in greater detail in Section~\ref{time_sync_cam}. Importantly for the discussion of distributed computation on Minion, the Jetson down-samples the video feeds from their native frame rate to 5 \ac{fps} before transmitting them over the network.
The reduction in frame rate is essential to maintain reliable network performance given limited bandwidth, as discussed in Section \ref{comp:network}. 
The maximum bitrate $R_{\text{max}}$ of each stream is computed at runtime as a function of the target frame rate, frame resolution, and compression ratio, defined by

\begin{equation}
    R_{\text{max}} = N_x N_y b f,
\end{equation}
where $N_x$ and $N_y$ are the image width and height in pixels, $f$ is the desired frame rate, and $b$ is the effective bits per pixel after compression.
For the HDR camera, the image resolution is $2880 \times 1860$ pixels, encoded in YUV 4:2:0 format using H.264 compression.
The parameter $b$ represents the effective number of bits per pixel after H.264 compression. 
This ratio is generally between 0.1 and 0.2 and is primarily dependent on the scene's visual complexity. 
Static or low-detail scenes compress more efficiently, while scenes with high motion or fine texture require more bits to maintain comparable visual quality. 
In operation, there is generally quite a lot of movement within the scene, but it is not very complex, with a large portion of the \ac{FOV} occupied by the sky and the water surface.
Empirical testing of the camera's stream during operation found a balance between quality and compression at $b=0.14$.
% An empirically derived constant of $b = 0.14$ bits per pixel captures the combined effects of color subsampling and compression efficiency under typical scene conditions.
These values yield a bitrate for the HDR camera of $\approx 3.75\; \text{Mbps}$ with the populated equation
\begin{equation}
        R_{\text{max}} = (2880 \times 1860\; \text{px}) (0.14 \;\text{bits/px})(5\;\text{s}^{-1})\;.
\end{equation}

This adaptive bitrate approach scales linearly with frame rate and ensures that each video stream remains within the available network bandwidth while maintaining adequate visual fidelity for perception and data logging.
By default, each camera operates at 5 \ac{fps}, aligning with the data rate of other perception sensors, but this rate can be adjusted in the future using this approach to ensure that the video streams do not saturate the network bandwidth. % to accommodate specific mission or testing requirements.

While compressed video is streamed to Atlas, the Jetson simultaneously records each feed at its native frame rate to the onboard 500 GB solid-state \ac{NVMe} drive as redundant backups. 
Each recording is saved in 120-second segments, along with a corresponding \ac{CSV} file that pairs each image frame with a timestamp from the system clock. 
The same system clock provides the \ac{PTP} signal used by the Livox LiDAR units for time synchronization.
This ensures consistent temporal alignment across all sensors within the perception suite. 
Consequently, the timestamps in the recorded \ac{CSV} files are also essential for validating the synchronization of the camera and LiDAR, as discussed in Section \ref{time_sync_cam}.

\section{Temporal Synchronization} \label{sec:calibration_temporal}

Spatial alignment was achieved while Minion was stationary, and all observed targets were also static. 
To maintain this level of accuracy once the vessel and targets are in motion, it becomes necessary to synchronize each sensor’s data in time as well. 

\subsection{Network Synchronization} 
\label{time_sync_lan}

The Minion platform employs a hierarchical time-synchronization system that distributes GPS-disciplined time from the PinPoint receiver to all perception sensors and computing nodes. 
The PinPoint GPS module serves as the master time source, and its clock is propagated across the local network via both \ac{NTP} and \acl{PTP} (PTP) services \cite{ptp2008}. 
% PTP standard covers NTP, PTP, and 1588 protocols
These two mechanisms work together to cascade timing information from the PinPoint GPS receiver, through the Atlas computing cluster, to the Jetson Xavier in the camera enclosure, and then to each Livox Horizon LiDAR unit (see Figure~\ref{fig:NTP_PTP}).
At the top of this chain, the PinPoint GPS module provides Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) with sub-microsecond accuracy via the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS). 
This external clock acts as the authoritative reference for the entire vessel, ensuring that all downstream systems inherit a consistent time base.

\subsubsection{Network Time Protocol}
Within Minion's \ac{LAN}, a Chrony \ac{NTP} daemon operates on the primary Atlas computer, serving as the authoritative time server for all onboard devices.
Chrony is a service that continuously disciplines the system clock using the GPS reference and corrects for minor drift or oscillatory instability \cite{chrony}.
The NVIDIA Jetson inside the camera enclosure operates as an \ac{NTP} client, synchronizing its system clock with the Atlas PC.
The resulting clock alignment achieves approximately 1–10 milliseconds of accuracy, which is sufficient for coordinating 5 Hz camera streams and maintaining consistent ROS timestamps across computing nodes.
The synchronization status is verified at startup by onboard scripts that query Chrony before any video or LiDAR processes begin. 
This verification ensures that recording never proceeds when clocks are unsynchronized.
% In addition, automated diagnostic checks allow operators to confirm that time distribution remains stable throughout offshore missions, where the GPS link represents the sole external time authority

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsubsection{Precision Time Protocol}
While \ac{NTP} provides millisecond-level precision for the system cameras and computing nodes, the system LiDAR sensors demand tighter synchronization. Each Livox Horizon operates at 100~Hz with microsecond-timestamped measurements, where even millisecond-level drift would cause measurable spatial distortion during vessel motion. To meet this requirement, the Jetson Xavier distributes the \ac{PTP} signal using the Linux ptp4l service.

In this configuration, the Jetson acts as the \ac{PTP} master clock server, acting as the primary time reference for the camera data, and broadcasting timing packets over the camera-enclosure network to all three Livox devices, which function as \ac{PTP} clients. The system clock acts \ac{PTP} uses hardware-timestamped Ethernet packets to measure network delay symmetrically and correct for it in real time, achieving sub-microsecond synchronization between LiDAR units.
The PTP signal can be manually confirmed on each Livox device using the Livox Viewer utility, which verifies synchronization with an “active 1588 signal” message once the signal is established.


\subsection{Camera Time Synchronization} \label{time_sync_cam}

Without a valid timestamp for each acquired video frame, the camera data cannot be spatially aligned with the LiDAR data.
LiDAR measurements are recorded in the map frame, in order to aggregate data over time. Therefore, knowing the moment that each video frame was captured is essential for determining the correct vehicle pose at the moment the camera and LiDAR data need aligning. 
This requirement becomes more challenging when video must be transmitted between two separate computers, as is the case with Minion. 
ROS does not support compressed video formats, so real-time video transfer must be handled outside the ROS message layer. 
GStreamer provides an effective solution, and prior work has shown that it is well-suited for high-throughput streaming \cite{thompson2023}. 
However, GStreamer pipelines do not preserve per-frame timing information, and conventional video streaming protocols maintain only relative timing within the stream rather than the absolute acquisition timestamps needed for aligning measurements across sensors. 
The work presented here reconstructs this missing timing information by embedding precise timestamps directly into the video encoding and recovering them after decoding on the receiving computer. 
This allows the video stream to maintain frame-level temporal fidelity despite network latency or dropped frames.
For this method to be effective, the two system clocks must also be synchronized.

% This implementation replaces the caption-based approach with direct timestamp embedding using \ac{SEI} \ac{NAL} units defined in the H.264/H.265 standard \cite{wiegand2003}.
Each video frame is assigned a GPS-disciplined system timestamp from the NVIDIA Jetson at the moment of capture. The timestamp is stored as a 64-bit Unix-epoch millisecond count and attached to the frame through a custom GStreamer metadata extension. Embedding the timestamp within the same encoded video layer transmitted over \ac{RTSP} ensures that temporal information remains bound to each frame, even when network buffering or frame loss occurs. The complete video processing pipeline is shown in Figure~\ref{fig_video_pipeline}.

In the final configuration, GStreamer ingests the raw video stream and records the GPS-disciplined capture timestamp as custom metadata. The pipeline then forks into three branches. 
In the first branch, the stream is encoded at the native frame rate and saved to an MP4 file. In another branch, the original GStreamer plugin developed by Thompson \cite{thompson2023} is used to index each processed image frame with the system clock time in a CSV file.
The third branch performs the new process developed for real-time transmission. 
The camera’s native frame rate is downsampled to a user-defined frame rate, and encoded using the Jetson’s hardware-accelerated H.264 encoders. During encoding, the metadata timestamp is inserted into the SEI payload, following which, the resulting H.264 stream is packetized as RTP and transmitted to the designated network endpoint.
The combination of these three pipeline branches ensures that both synchronized video streaming is available for real-time use and a high-fidelity copy is saved locally for later analysis. 

On Minion, the Atlas PC decodes incoming streams using a new and complementary GStreamer pipeline equipped with a pad probe on the \texttt{h264parse} element. This probe identifies SEI NAL units and extracts the embedded timestamp before decoding the video frame. The image frame is then saved as a ROS \texttt{sensor\_msgs/Image} message, and the recovered timestamp is saved to the message header before it is published to the rest of the ROS network.

This revised GStreamer architecture represents a central technical contribution of this work. It provides a mechanism for high-rate video acquisition, precise frame-level timestamping, and reliable network transport within a modular camera enclosure operating on edge-level hardware, enabling the downstream temporal alignment required for any visual-perception decision-making.

\subsection{YOLO} \label{yolo}

% Purpose and Context
The eighth generation of the \ac{YOLO} framework was selected to provide a baseline performance for real-time vision-based detection in this research. 
This method of detection gained popularity for its ease of use and implementation for detecting and localizing objects within an image \cite{terven2023}.
Once an object is detected, the pixel coordinates it occupies within the image frame are bounded with a rectangle known as the bounding box (see Figure \ref{fig:YOLO_tower}).
Its open-source models are pretrained on the COCO dataset, a large repository containing approximately 1.5 million labeled images, and are provided in various sizes and qualities, which can be deployed on a wide variety of edge computing hardware.
Additionally, transfer learning is simple to apply, allowing these models to be tailored for more specific use cases, such as maritime environments.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{Images/YOLO_ex1.png}
    \caption{Rectangular bounding boxes placed around maritime objects detected by a re-trained YOLO detection model.}
    \label{fig:YOLO_tower}
\end{figure}

The name YOLO refers to the single-shot \ac{CNN} architecture, which performs both detection and localization in a single pass.
While this design was key to achieving real-time detection speeds, the accuracy of an object’s bounding box within an image is limited by the available training data. 
Prior to the release of version 8 in 2023, the model required that the center and bounds of each detected object be explicitly encoded within the model, requiring a large corpus of labeled data for training.
Version 8 introduced dual detection heads at the end of the CNN architecture: the first head identifies if and where an object is present within the image, while the second head determines the object's bounding box.
This “anchor-free” architectural change not only improves the reliability of object localization but also reduces the burden of transfer learning required for fine-tuning pre-trained models in earlier versions \cite{terven2023, ultralytics}.

The widespread adoption and speed of YOLOv8 make it the optimal choice for providing a baseline visual detection method, and the fundamental architectural changes in this version validate its selection for a late-fusion approach.

\subsection{GB-CACHE} \label{gbcache}
%\subfile{sections/gbcache}

Grid-Based Clustered Adaptive Concave Hull Extraction (GB-CACHE) is a spatial indexing and clustering methodology specifically designed for efficient processing of three-dimensional point cloud data in maritime environments \cite{coyleEacceptedForPub}. 
The algorithm provides rapid object segmentation through grid-based spatial partitioning followed by concave-hull boundary extraction, enabling real-time detection performance essential for autonomous navigation safety.

\subsubsection{Grid-Based Spatial Indexing and Clustering} \label{gbcache_clustering}
% ### Grid-Based Spatial Indexing and Clustering

GB-CACHE operates on the principle of partitioning the three-dimensional LiDAR point cloud into a regular grid, which enables efficient spatial queries and proximity-based clustering. Using this grid-based approach, points are organized into fixed spatial bins.
Points that fall within the same grid cell, or adjacent cells, are considered spatially proximate, simplifying subsequent clustering.
This spatial indexing strategy is particularly advantageous for maritime LiDAR data due to characteristics of both the environment and sensor modality. LiDAR operating at 905 nm in the near-infrared is strongly absorbed by water, preventing most subsurface returns. Occasionaly, surface or subsurface returns can still occur due to specular reflections from sunlight, these points are infrequent and are easily filtered out, leaving point clouds that are sparse in open-water conditions. 
Under such sparsity, the grid-based structure used by GB-CACHE efficiently ignores large empty regions and concentrates computation only where points are present. 
This reduces memory usage and computational overhead while naturally segmenting candidate regions that may contain objects of interest, thereby streamlining the subsequent object detection operations.

% The fundamental principle underlying GB-CACHE is to partition the three-dimensional LiDAR point cloud into a regular grid, facilitating efficient spatial queries and proximity-based clustering. Rather than evaluating pairwise distances among all points, the grid-based approach achieves linear complexity by organizing points into fixed spatial bins. Each grid cell represents a discrete volumetric region, and points falling within the same cell or adjacent cells are considered spatially proximate.
% This spatial indexing approach proves particularly advantageous for maritime LiDAR data. 
% The $905 nm$ wavelength of LIDAR is in the near-infrared part of the electromagnetic spectrum and is strongly absorbed by water, effectively suppressing subsurface returns.
% This means that only points reflected by the water surface are returned with an intensity greater than zero, making it straightforward to filter out points from the ground plane.
% The sparse nature of maritime point clouds enables the grid structure to efficiently isolate regions containing potential objects while discarding empty space, thereby reducing both memory consumption and computational overhead.

% ### Concave Hull Extraction and Feature Calculation
\subsubsection{Concave Hull and Feature Extraction} \label{gbcache_features}

Within the GB CACHE framework, each newly detected cluster is assigned a sequentially incremented object ID. 
This ID persists as long as the object remains continuously within the autonomous system's field of view.
% However, if an object leaves the sensor’s field of view due to occlusion or other factors for longer than five seconds, it is assigned a new object ID upon re-detection. IDs also will not be retained across separate ROS bag files or after a restart of the GB-CACHE node.
For each new object identified, a concave hull is created to define the object's spatial extent. 
While convex hulls guarantee computational efficiency and provide simple bounding representations, concave hulls more accurately capture object shape by allowing inward boundary curvature. 
This distinction proves critical for maritime objects with non-convex geometries, including U-shaped channel markers or vessels with significant superstructure features.

The concave hull generation process balances boundary accuracy against computational cost through a configurable complexity parameter that controls the degree of boundary concavity. 
The resulting polygon provides a compact geometric representation suitable for both visualization and feature extraction for subsequent classification. 
Beyond simple bounding boxes, concave hull boundaries enable the calculation of geometric features, such as area and perimeter, that support object classification. 


While the GB-CACHE method supports multiple classification methods \cite{coyleE, thompson2017}, a \ac{MGC} was used at the 2024 RobotX competition. 

\subsubsection{Multivariate Gaussian Classifier} \label{gbcache_mgc}
% A multivariate Gaussian classifier was selected to be paired with GB-CACHE for object classification. 
The object-classification stage of GB-CACHE employs a multivariate Gaussian classifier (MGC) to model the joint distribution of the extracted LiDAR features for each object category. The method assumes that the features describing objects are continuous and approximately Gaussian within each class. 
Under this assumption, each class is represented by a mean feature vector and a full covariance matrix estimated from labeled training samples. 
With this model, the likelihood of an observed feature vector $\mathbf{f}$ given class $C_i$ is described by the multivariate Gaussian density,
\begin{equation}
p\left(\mathbf{f} \mid C_i\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2 \pi)^d\left|\Sigma_i\right|}} \exp \left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{f}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_i\right)^{\top} \Sigma_i^{-1}\left(\mathbf{f}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_i\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}_i$ and $\Sigma_i$ are the class-specific mean vector and covariance matrix, and $d$ is the dimensionality of the feature space.

During inference, the classifier evaluates this likelihood for each class and selects the class with the highest posterior probability under Bayes’ rule. This formulation captures correlations between features and provides computationally efficient inference. The resulting distributions enable GB-CACHE to assign incoming objects to the most likely class or to identify observations that fall sufficiently far from all known class distributions as ``unknown.'' 

A total of ten features are used for classification. Four intensity-based features are derived directly from the segmented LiDAR returns, and six geometric features are computed from the object’s height, concave hull, and occupancy-grid representation. These features, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:gbcache_features}, provide a compact description of the object’s reflectivity characteristics, shape, and spatial structure. 

Thompson previously implemented GB-CACHE with an\ac{MGC} using the same the ten features listed in Table~\ref{tab:gbcache_features} \cite{thompson2017}. In that work the classifier was applied empirically without evaluating how well the individual features satisfied the Gaussian assumption. A detailed assessment of these feature behaviors and their influence on model accuracy is provided in Section~\ref{features_gbcache}.

\section{Real-Time Performance}
% \paragraph{Real-Time Performance}

To ensure a consistent and meaningful comparison between the two real-time detection pipelines, runtime was selected as a primary performance metric. 
Processor and memory utilization were not chosen because the two methods rely on fundamentally different hardware and memory architectures: the neural network–based YOLO executes on the \ac{GPU} with high-bandwidth \ac{vRAM}, whereas GB-CACHE operates entirely on the \ac{CPU} and conventional system memory and significantly lower memory bandwidth.
To be effective for real-time use, both pipelines must effectively process data faster than they receive it.
This performance evaluation of both detectors was conducted using all 98 ROS bag files containing synchronized camera and LiDAR data on Minion's hardware. Data was played back at real-time speeds using the ROS parameter \texttt{use\_sim\_time = true}.

\subsection{GB-CACHE Performance} \label{sec:gbcache_results}

To evaluate computational performance, the GB-CACHE processing sequence is grouped into three subroutines.
These subroutines correspond to the sequential stages that handle LiDAR data input, spatial filtering, and map updating during each processing cycle.
The first subroutine, \texttt{filter\_points}, removes points reflected from the water's surface, reducing noise and false positives with object detection.
The second routine, \texttt{add\_points}, assigns the remaining raw LiDAR returns to the occupancy grid, where they await further processing.
The final subroutine, \texttt{map\_points}, combines segmentation, clustering, and classification.
Figure~\ref{fig:gbcache_flow_analysis} provides a block diagram of the GB-CACHE workflow with these three subroutines windowing the related tasks.

To assess performance, all ROS bags were replayed on the same Minion PC hardware that recorded them, providing only the raw LiDAR data and navigational topics to the GB-CACHE ROS node. 
This approach simulates the operational data processing environment. 
The execution time of each subroutine was measured along with the number of points processed by each function. 
Overall, more than 1.7 million executions of \texttt{filter\_points} and \texttt{add\_points} and more than 45 thousand executions of \texttt{update\_map} were measured across the entire dataset.

The LiDAR data is transmitted at 100 Hz from three Livox Horizon units, while the ROS topic \texttt{obj\_w\_class} generated by GB-CACHE is published at 5 Hz. 
The first two subroutines examined, \texttt{filter\_points} and \texttt{add\_points}, execute sequentially without parallelization. 
These processes must handle raw data from all three LiDAR units at 100 Hz.
Therefore, the summed total execution time of these two subroutines must not exceed 3.33 milliseconds.
The \texttt{update\_map} subroutine receives the outputs of \texttt{filter\_points} and \texttt{add\_points} but runs asynchronously and outputs detections only at 5 Hz, providing an allotted execution time of 200 milliseconds per cycle.

By fitting a line to both scatter plots, we see that the computational time follows a quadratic trend, while the perimeter plot follows a linear trend.
This is in line with the author’s estimation of system complexity $O(n \times m)$ in the case where $m <<n$ and the complexity approaches linear complexity of $O(n)$ \cite{coyleE}, where $n$ denotes the number of occupied grid cells being examined and $m$ represents the number of neighboring cells checked for each $n$.
The quadratic trend we see in Figure~\ref{fig:gbcache_update_map_scatter_area} reflects the geometric relationship between perimeter and area. 
For compact shapes, area increases with the square of a characteristic linear dimension, whereas perimeter increases linearly. 
Because the algorithm’s cost is effectively determined by perimeter length, plotting execution time against area naturally yields an upward-sloping trend that aligns with the observed quadratic fit.
This means that the efficiency with which GB-CACHE processes and populates the map grows linearly with boundary complexity.

\subsection{Single-Mode Detection Comparison} \label{yolo_gbcache_compare}

A primary distinction between the two methods lies in the effort required to produce a functional classifier. 
The YOLO model relies on transfer learning and therefore requires a labeled image dataset.
For this study, 778 images containing 1,934 manually annotated bounding boxes were generated, requiring approximately 13 hours of labeling. 
Acquiring datasets that capture the environmental variability and lighting makes is still a time consuming challenge to accuratly reflect the environment.

In contrast, GB-CACHE requires only that tracked LiDAR objects be associated with a class label.
From 10,984 LiDAR detections, consolidation into tracked objects produced 916 final training samples across six classes.
The complete data extraction, feature computation, and classifier training for all six classes were completed in under an hour. 
This low training burden is a significant advantage for rapid deployment and for operations in environments where labeled vision data is scarce, especially considering training can be performed during operation.

% \subsection{Computational Performance and Real-Time Feasibility}

Although YOLO and GB-CACHE both achieve real-time performance, they do so under different computational assumptions.
YOLOv8 inference was executed on the NVIDIA GTX 1080 installed in Atlas, achieving an average latency of 12.1ms.
GB-CACHE, by contrast, operates entirely on the CPU, and its runtime remained comfortably below real-time requirements across all samples tested. The underpinning deterministic structure scales linearly with scene complexity and the perimeter of detected objects, rather than the number of observed points.
The speed and clustering accuracy it achieves make it an attractive choice for hardware-constrained systems and for safety-critical systems where predictable performance is essential for obstacle avoidance and planning.

% \subsection{Differences in Classification Capability}

The two modalities provide fundamentally different types of information.
YOLO performs both detection and classification using two-dimensional visual cues such as color, texture, and shape. Its mAP50 performance of approximately 0.80 reflects the combined quality of localization and semantic assignment, but it is sensitive to lighting, color saturation, and glare. Because YOLO has traditionally coupled detection and classification, its effectiveness depended heavily on the diversity of the labeled images used for transfer learning. The multi-head architectural change seen in YOLOv8 reduces the number of samples needed to localize objects, but achieving robust classification in maritime environments remains challenging due to the wide variability of environmental factors. As a result, collecting a dataset that adequately represents real-world conditions is still a significant obstacle to building a broadly reliable vision-based detector.

GB-CACHE performs classification only after geometric clustering.
Its multivariate Gaussian classifier achieved 97.49\% accuracy across six classes, but this figure reflects only semantic class assignment, not object detection performance.
GB-CACHE’s reliance on three-dimensional geometry enables it to distinguish between classes that present similar visual appearance but different LiDAR signatures.
This is most clearly evident in the A2 and A3 buoys: YOLO cannot reliably distinguish them due to their similar appearance in 2D imagery and must therefore treat them as a combined \texttt{round\_buoy} class, while GB-CACHE uses geometric measurements within the point cloud to retain them as separate categories.

The complementary strengths of YOLO and GB-CACHE highlight why neither sensing modality is sufficient on its own for robust perception in maritime environments.
YOLO provides rich semantic information and strong classification performance under favorable lighting conditions but remains sensitive to illumination extremes and limited data availability, while offering limited 3D spatial fidelity.
GB-CACHE is still sensitive to direct sunlight. It also excels at geometric discrimination but cannot infer color-based attributes beyond simple reflectivity approximations and depends on LiDAR visibility, which may be reduced under heavy conditions.These findings clarify the roles each system plays: YOLO excels in visual classification, and GB-CACHE excels in geometric discrimination. Understanding these differences is essential when evaluating performance across sensing modalities.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Sample Independence}

The raw dataset comprised over 1.2 million LiDAR observations collected over the 98 total bags. After filtering to exclude unknown classifications, 441,860 observations of positively classified objects remained. However, these observations are not independent samples. Each unique object was observed across multiple consecutive frames as it was tracked by the sensor platform. To ensure statistical independence required for mutual information estimation, feature values for each unique object instance were computed by averaging across all temporal observations of that object, identified by the object's unique tracking identifier.

% This temporal averaging approach addresses the pseudo-replication problem inherent in sequential sensor observations. 
% Multiple observations of the same object over 15 to 30 seconds are highly temporally correlated and violate the independence assumption underlying mutual information estimation. By computing one representative feature vector per unique physical object, samples are guaranteed to be independent by construction, and the analysis focuses on between-object discriminative power rather than within-object temporal variation. This averaging strategy is particularly well-suited to the GB-CACHE framework because of the Gaussian assumption utilized by the \ac{MGC},
% % (and verified in section~\ref{}). 
% Under the Gaussian assumption, the sample mean is a sufficient statistic for characterizing the central tendency of each object's feature distribution, making temporal averaging a statistically appropriate method for condensing multiple correlated observations into a single representative measurement.

\subsection{Mutual Information}
  
\Ac{MI} quantifies the reduction in uncertainty about one variable given knowledge of another, and is measured in bits \cite{shannon1948}. 
For this context, it provides a basis for evaluating the relationships among GB-CACHE features and class labels, and also between feature pairs, and is calculated using 
\begin{equation}
I(X;Y)
= \sum_{x \in X} \sum_{y \in Y}
p(x, y)
\log \left( \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x) p(y)} \right).
\end{equation}

While $X$ and $Y$ can be any two random variables, they both must be either continuous or discrete. In this examination, the object classes are a discrete set of labels, whereas the features are continuous. 

% First, the \ac{MI} between each continuous feature $X$ and the discrete class label $Y$ was computed to determine how informative the feature is for distinguishing between object classes. 
% This calculation measures how strongly variations in each feature reduce uncertainty in the associated class label and therefore reflects each feature's individual contribution to object identification. 
% When comparing continuous and discrete variables, the continuous variables must be discretized into discrete probability mass functions using an appropriate binning strategy.
In this examination, the object classes are a discrete set of labels, whereas the features are continuous. First, the \ac{MI} between each continuous feature $X$ and the discrete class label $Y$ was computed to determine how informative the feature is for distinguishing between object classes. This calculation measures how strongly variations in each feature reduce uncertainty in the associated class label and therefore reflects each feature's individual contribution to object identification.

When comparing continuous and discrete variables, the continuous features are discretized into probability mass functions using a histogram-based binning strategy. Sturges’ Rule \cite{sturges1926} defines an appropriate number of bins for $N$ samples as
\begin{equation}
    n_{bins} = 1+log_2(N)\;,
\end{equation}

While the Light Tower and John Boat classes show strong redundancy among the geometric features $f_{6...10}$, these classes are significantly underrepresented in the dataset. Taken together, these results show that redundancy among the GB-CACHE features is structured, heterogeneous, and in some cases unexpectedly asymmetric. The intensity-derived features form a clear dependency group in both the global and class-conditional analyses, while the geometric features exhibit more varied relationships, including cases such as the A2 and A3 buoys where the occupied-grid-count feature $f_6$ aligns more strongly with one axis-length measurement than the other. These patterns illustrate that individual feature relevance does not necessarily align with redundancy structure and that some features carry overlapping information in ways not visible from relevance analysis alone.

The intensity-derived features provide both the highest discriminative value and the strongest redundancy, appearing consistently across the dataset and within individual classes. Because of this, it is reasonable to examine whether a smaller subset of them can retain most of the discriminatory power. \subsection{Optimal Feature Subsets}

The preceding relevance and redundancy analyses showed that several GB-CACHE features provide strong discriminative value, while others share substantial overlap, particularly within the intensity-derived group.
To evaluate How the interconnectivity of relevancy and redundancy combine to create a maximally efficient feature set. It may also be useful to identify low relevant and high redundant features to remove. in favor of a new feature that may be more discriminative and or less redundant. 

The process begins by selecting the single feature with the highest mutual information with the class labels. After the first feature has been chosen, the method proceeds iteratively. At iteration $t$, the set $S$ contains the $t-1$ features already selected. Every remaining candidate feature $f_k$ is scored by comparing its relevance to the class labels against its average redundancy with all features currently in $S$. The score is computed as
\begin{equation}
\text{mRMR}(f_k) = I(f_k; Y) - \frac{1}{|S|} \sum_{f_i \in S} I(f_k; f_i)
\end{equation}

where $I(f_k; Y)$ measures the mutual-information relevance of $f_k$, and the second term penalizes redundancy by computing its average mutual information with the existing subset.

The composition of this subset is informative. The selection of $f_9$ is less obvious at first glance, ranking in the middle of all ten features. In other words, features with the highest individual mutual information are not always the most valuable additions to a subset that already contains other strong features.

Finally, the decline of the mRMR score beyond three features does not, by itself, argue for reducing GB-CACHE to this minimal subset. The classifier already operates with low computational cost, and the redundant features may improve robustness under variations in environment, sensor noise, or future object categories that are not represented in the current dataset. In this context, the mRMR results serve primarily to characterize how information is distributed across the feature set. They show that a compact core of features dominates the discriminative performance, while the remaining features carry overlapping information that may still be valuable for stability and generalization.

Having established the capabilities and limitations of both vision and LiDAR-based detection systems in maritime environments, this section describes the implementation of a late-fusion method that combines their outputs at the decision level. 
The goal is to use the demonstrated

\subsection{Fusion Reference Frame Selection} \label{fusion_ref_frame}
The proposed late-fusion approach uses a Kalman gain to combine independent object detections from YOLO and GB-CACHE. 
This formulation allows each modality to influence the fused result in proportion to its confidence. 
Consequently, selecting a common reference frame is essential to apply the filter, ensuring accurate comparison and appropriate weighting of detections.

Three candidate reference frames are available. The LiDAR reference frame, the map frame, and the image frame. The LiDAR reference frame is not a suitable choice because LiDAR data is stored in the map frame for navigation. 
The map frame also presents challenges, as detections made in the image frame cannot be easily projected into three-dimensional space without additional depth information.
It is much easier to project into the image frame.
Consequently, the image frame is selected as the most practical and effective reference frame for late fusion.

\subsection{ROI Projection and Bias Estimation}

Object information recorded by GB-CACHE is stored as a series of two dimensional points in the map frame. To obtain a height and width oriented bounding box in the image frame. This flat object must be extruded vertically to the object's true height before it is projected onto the image frame. An illustration of this procedure is provided in Figure~\ref{fig:gbcach_roi}.

occupancy grid using its center coordinates. The vertices of the convex hull are also stored

concave hull from the water surface to the height of the object, we can create a

The top and bottom vertices of the polygon are then projected into the image frame and expressed as pixel coordinates.

This process yields a LiDAR-derived ROI expressed in the same pixel-based coordinate system as the YOLO-provided ROI, which can now be compared..

Because GB-CACHE is built on an occupancy grid, each hull vertex aligns to the intersection of an occupancy grid cell after discretization. 
This structure creates an inherent spatial uncertainty. 
Any object mapped to the grid can lie anywhere within the cell that contains its points, with a maximum error equal to the grid resolution, $r=0.1$ meters, and an expected error of 0.05 m in the horizontal directions. 
This creates an uncertainty for the ROI bounds in the image, which can be seen in the rightmost portion of Figure~\ref{fig:gbcach_roi}, where the representation of the object in the image frame contains a nontrivial amount of positional error of the red bounding box. 
The maximum uncertainty occurs when an object is viewed by Minion at an angle of 45 degrees relative to the occupation grid axes. 
The average uncertainty, therefore, occurs at an angle of 22.5°, giving an offset distance of
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{offset}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}+\sqrt{2r^2}\right) .
\end{equation}
For a grid dimension of $r = 0.1$ meters, this corresponds to an offset of approximately 0.0957 meters.
This uncertainty is corrected by adjusting the left and right edges of the resulting bounding box, based on the object's relative position in the field of view. This estimate reflects only the uncertainty introduced by the use of an occupancy grid and by projection effects when mapping grid points into the image frame. It does not quantify any residual error or bias induced through sensor calibration or temporal misalignment.

As this offset appears in the image plane, it can be directly compared with the possible uncertainty introduced by the image scaling step used in the YOLO method. Therefore, the uncertainty produced by YOLO has a theoretical limit that is fixed across all distances, set by the ratio of the image's horizontal resolution to the rescaled size of 640 pixels, resulting in a constant theoretical error of 4.48 pixels (4.0 pixels). 
Conversely, the uncertainty created by the projection method of GB-CACHE is a fixed spatial dimension that scales in the image plane with distance to the camera. 
By plotting the uncertainty generated by both models as a function of object distance (see Figure~\ref{fig:gbcach_error_theoretical}), we see that GB-CACHE should have lower theoretical uncertainty than YOLO for objects that are 29.05 meters away or further.

\subsection{Detection Association}
The fusion node evaluates potential detection matches each time an image is received from the HDR camera. The image callback waits for both a YOLO detection message and a GB-CACHE detection message, which are always published regardless of whether detections are present.

The YOLO detection message perpetuates the exact timestamp of the image it processed to ensure this information stays aligned.
However, while GB-CACHE publishes data at the same rate as the camera, the data from each is published asynchronously. The fusion node waits for the first GB-CACHE message whose timestamp is later than both the image timestamp and the YOLO timestamp.
This ensures that ROS can interpolate between odometry messages to obtain Minion's pose at the moment the image was captured, and the necessary projection from the map frame can be performed.

Once all required inputs are available, the fusion node determines whether an object detected by YOLO corresponds to a compatible detection made by GB-CACHE. Although class compatibility can be used to limit the search space, it is not strictly required.
In practice, all ROIs are compared, and candidate ``parent'' ROIs which have an IoU threshold of $\geq 0.7$ are selected, paired, and passed to the fusion algorithm. 

If multiple ``parent'' candidates meet these criteria, pairs with the highest IoU are selected, and the center-to-center distance is used to break any ties.
If either of these conditions fails, the labels used for this method are still available for other system tasks.
YOLO is unable to spatially condition its detections to the desired range of 60 meters
Therefore, single-mode detections are intentionally ignored.
This rule-based association permits graceful degradation in scenarios where one sensing modality fails, or the spatial calibration or temporal synchronization becomes unreliable.

These conditions ensure that fusion is performed only when both methods provide consistent and mutually corroborating observations.

\subsection{Offline Confidence Weighting}
During calibration, only detection pairs with ground-truth ROIs are used to estimate the per-edge error statistics for YOLO and GB-CACHE. 
The reliability of each sensing modality is characterized offline by comparing the ROI of each parent bounding box to a manually-labeled ground truth ROI. The mean and variance of this error are used to inform how much influence each model will have on the fusion process, similar to the gain of a Kalman filter. 
Estimation is used in the calculation of the predicted result discussed in the next section (~\ref{Kalman_Weighted_Fusion}).

Each ROI pair that enters the late-fusion pipeline is defined by two pixel coordinates ($x_1$, $y_1$) and ($x_2$, $y_2$) that specify the top-left and bottom-right corners of the bounding box.
These  four values correspond to the left, top, right, and bottom edges of the bounding-box label generated by sensing method $S$,
\begin{equation}
X_{S} = (L_{S}, T_{S}, R_{S}, B_{S}),
\end{equation}
where $S \in \{\text{LiDAR}, \text{YOLO}\}$ denotes the source, and 
$L_S$, $T_S$, $R_S$, and $B_S$ are the left, top, right, and bottom pixel coordinates. 
Therefore, the error between $X_{S}$ and a set of ground-truth ROI labels $X_{GT}$ is defined
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{E}_{S} = X_{S} - X_{\text{GT}}
\end{equation}

By assuming that the distributions of errors $\mathbf{E}_S$ will trend towards a normal distribution  ($\mathcal{N}(\mu , \sigma^2)$) for a sufficiently large set of samples $N$, the error for each state in $X$ is characterized as  
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\mu} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} E_{i}\\
\mathbf{\sigma}^2 &= \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
\bigl(E_{i} - \mu\bigr)^2
\end{align}

Although the errors are not guaranteed to be strictly Gaussian, inspection across the dataset (see Figure~\ref{per_edge_error_yolo}) revealed unimodal and approximately symmetric error profiles, supporting the use of the mean-adjusted model for variance-based weighting.

Sources with less variance are more consistent with ground truth and should receive a higher weight in the fused estimate, and are generally calculated as
% \begin{equation}
% w = \frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}
% \end{equation}
\begin{align}
\mathbf{w}_\text{YOLO} &=\frac{\sigma_\text{LiDAR}^2}{\sigma_\text{YOLO}^2+\sigma_\text{LiDAR}^2},\\
\mathbf{w}_\text{LiDAR}&=\frac{\sigma_\text{YOLO}^2}{\sigma_\text{YOLO}^2+\sigma_\text{LiDAR}^2}.
\end{align}

\subsection{Kalman Weighted Fusion} \label{Kalman_Weighted_Fusion}

Because temporal propagation is not required for this application, the Kalman gain is applied in its static form, where the fused measurement is the variance-weighted combination of two independent observations.
Finally, the bounds of a fused ROI can be expressed as a weighted average between two detection modes $S_1$ and $S_2$ with the equation
% \begin{equation}
% X_{\text{fused}} =
% \frac{
% (X_{S_1}-\bar{\mu}_{S_1}) \, \bar{w}_{S_2}
% +
% (X_{S_2}-\bar{\mu}_{S_2}) \, \bar{w}_{X,S_1}
% }{
% \bar{w}_{X,S_1} + \bar{w}_{S_2}
% }

% \end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{R O I}^*=\left(\boldsymbol{R O I _ { \text{LiDAR} }}-\mu_\text{LiDAR}\right) \mathbf{w}_\text{YOLO}+\left(\boldsymbol{R O I _ { \text{YOLO} }}-\mu_\text{YOLO}\right) \mathbf{w}_\text{LiDAR} \, , \label{Kalman_correction}
\end{equation}
where the weight applied to one mode's detected edge ($\boldsymbol{w}_1$) is based upon the variance of the same edge of the other mode ($\boldsymbol{w}_2$).

\chapter{Conclusions} \label{chapter_conclusions}

This dissertation addressed the critical gap in quantitative performance comparison between LiDAR and vision-based sensing for real-time maritime object detection on uncrewed surface vessels. The research established a unified experimental framework using ERAUs Minion \ac{USV} platform to evaluate the real-time performance of YOLOv8 and the deterministic LiDAR-based GB-CACHE system across six maritime object categories. Beyond modality-specific evaluation, this work introduced and validated a computationally efficient and real-time late-fusion strategy that improves detection stability and accuracy beyond what either sensing modality achieves independently.

The research achieved four primary accomplishments that represent novel contributions to maritime autonomy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Development of new sensor calibration methodologies that achieve sub-microsecond temporal synchronization and high-fidelity spatial alignment.
    \item Quantitative comparison of neural network and deterministic methods for real-time object detection, establishing operational burdens, computational predictability, and fundamental trade-offs.
    \item Information-theoretic feature analysis of the GB-CACHE classifier, revealing redundancy structure and latent capacity for classification expansion.
    \item Validation of a computationally efficient and real-time late-fusion framework that produces variance-optimal and unbiased bounding-box estimates.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Accomplishments}
\subsection{Novel Sensor Calibration: Spatial and Temporal}

This research established the foundation for reliable multi-modal perception by developing precise and reproducible calibration techniques. The spatial alignment of the sensor suite began with the intrinsic calibration of the high dynamic range camera, which achieved a mean reprojection error of 0.63 pixels. A new iterative approach was developed for the LiDAR-to-camera extrinsic calibration that refined both extrinsic and intrinsic parameters by aligning geometric features across both sensing modalities. This method ensured spatial fidelity and enabled accurate projection of LiDAR returns onto structures at twice the intended operational envelope of 60 meters.

The temporal synchronization represents one of the major contributions of this dissertation. The process begins with a GPS-disciplined system clock that serves as the authoritative time source for the platform. This clock is propagated across the computing devices through NTP and refined through PTP to achieve sub-microsecond alignment between the systems and the Livox LiDAR units. This unified time base ensures that all sensors operate on a consistent and deterministic temporal framework. Building on this synchronized foundation, a software pipeline was developed that embeds the system time directly into the H.264 and H.265 video bitstream through Supplemental Enhancement Information. This mechanism attaches the correct timestamp to each video frame at the moment of capture and preserves that timing through buffering, network delays, and frame drops. Because this approach relies only on the system clock and standard video-encoding features, it is fully agnostic to operating system, hardware platform, and camera interface. It can be integrated into any software that produces encoded video streams, enabling reliable cross-system temporal alignment for diverse robotic architectures. The combined system provides accurate frame-level timing for both camera and LiDAR data.

This contribution enables precise and repeatable temporal alignment across heterogeneous perception systems and supports the development of advanced time-sensitive fusion algorithms.


\subsection{Quantitative Comparison of Real-Time Detection Performance}

This research provided the empirical basis for evaluating the differences between YOLOv8 and GB-CACHE in both computational and behavioral performance. YOLOv8 required a manually annotated dataset of 778 images and a significant amount of transfer learning effort. The combined annotation and fine-tuning process required approximately thirteen hours. In contrast, GB-CACHE required minimal labeled data and relied on geometric characterization. The entire data collection and training process, including multivariate Gaussian classifier training, was completed in less than two hours.

Both detectors satisfied the required real-time constraint of completing inference within 200 milliseconds. YOLOv8 achieved a consistent average latency of 12.1 milliseconds per frame, while GB-CACHE demonstrated an  average of 8.002 milliseconds with its update stage with more variability. However, the observed behavior of the GB-CACHE pipeline was consistent with the near-linear scaling described in prior work. This agreement provides external validation of the underlying method while confirming its suitability for deployment on real-time maritime platforms.

The quantitative evaluation served a wider purpose than characterizing detector performance. It provided the groundwork that enabled the late-fusion architecture to be built on top of these two modalities. By identifying the strengths, limitations, and computational profiles of both systems, the analysis revealed the complementary structure required for an effective fusion strategy. The fusion method relies directly on these measured properties, and its development would not have been possible without the empirical understanding gained through this comparison.

This comparison provides a foundation for selecting sensing strategies that meet the computational and reliability needs of safety-critical maritime systems and supports the integration of these modalities into a unified fusion framework.

\subsection{GB-CACHE Feature Analysis}

This dissertation presented the first information-theoretic evaluation of the ten features used by the GB-CACHE multivariate Gaussian classifier. The analysis revealed strong redundancy among several intensity-derived features. The minimum-redundancy maximum-relevance criterion identified an optimal subset of three features: the standard deviation of intensity, the minimum intensity, and the minor axis length of the concave hull. The inclusion of the minor axis length was unexpected because it did not rank among the top features by individual mutual information. Its selection showed that the most effective subset is defined by complementary structure rather than raw relevance ranking.

The analysis demonstrated that the current ten-feature system is underutilized for the given dataset and that GB-CACHE has capacity to accommodate additional classes without compromising efficiency or classification performance.
This result supports principled expansion of the GB-CACHE classifier to more diverse maritime targets and enables feature-driven adaptation for broader mission profiles.

\subsection{Validation of Computationally Efficient Late Fusion}

This work culminated in the development of a lightweight late-fusion strategy that integrates YOLOv8 and GB-CACHE detections through a variance-weighted Kalman update. The method assigns weights to each modality based on the characterization of its error and produces fused bounding-box edges that reduce variance while maintaining unbiased estimates. The evaluation demonstrated consistent improvements in stability and accuracy across all object classes included in the dataset.

The broader significance of the fusion method extends beyond the immediate experimental results. The fusion mechanism is closed form and requires only a small number of scalar operations per edge, making it an attractive option for embedded or resource-limited systems. The approach generalizes to any pair of independent detection modalities and provides a modular design pattern for integrating additional sensors or learning-based detectors into future perception stacks. It also establishes a framework that can support dynamic filtering and state estimation approaches in later system iterations.

This contribution enables reliable decision-level integration in real time and provides a foundation for more advanced fusion architectures that incorporate adaptive filtering and predictive modeling.

This dissertation established a reliable foundation for multi-modal maritime perception by developing precise calibration methods, characterizing the behavior of vision and LiDAR detection systems, and demonstrating a lightweight late-fusion approach that improves the stability and accuracy of object boundaries. The results also suggest several clear opportunities for refining and extending the system, and these opportunities form a natural path forward for both applied development and continued research.

% The first area of advancement involves temporal integration. The fusion method introduced in this work operates on individual frames with static variance weighting. Although this produces unbiased and stable estimates, future systems will benefit from incorporating temporal continuity. A dynamic fusion method that updates the fused estimate over time and adjusts its confidence based on recent error behavior would improve robustness under difficult conditions such as partial occlusions, intermittent modality failure, and dynamic maritime environments. This capability would also allow fused detections to remain stable even when only one modality provides a reliable measurement at a given moment.

The first area of advancement involves extending the fusion process from a frame-by-frame operation to a temporally integrated estimation method. A temporally recursive version of the method would update the fused bounding-box estimate and its associated gains over time as new measurements arrive, allowing the filter to adjust confidence dynamically based on recent performance. Doing so would enable the system to propagate prior fused estimates forward, incorporate new measurements as they arrive, and adjust confidence dynamically based on recent error trends. Temporal integration would improve resilience to partial occlusions, intermittent modality dropouts, and rapidly changing maritime conditions by allowing the fused estimate to remain stable even when only a single modality provides a reliable observation. This approach would transform the fusion process from static variance weighting into a full spatiotemporal estimator capable of maintaining continuous, high-confidence object bounds.

Related to temporal fusion is the opportunity to implement object-level tracking. GB-CACHE already assigns persistent identifiers to LiDAR clusters, and these identifiers can anchor objects across frames and support associations with camera detections. Extending this capability by incorporating ego-motion information from the navigation system would enable object velocity estimation, trajectory prediction, and continuity of object identities in cluttered or rapidly changing scenes. Together, temporal fusion and object tracking would transform the perception system from a frame-wise detector into a predictive sensing framework suitable for more advanced autonomous behaviors.

The feature analysis performed in this dissertation also revealed important opportunities for classifier expansion. The GB-CACHE method demonstrated strong performance despite relying on only a subset of the available features. This indicates that the classifier has additional capacity for new maritime object classes without increasing computational cost. Future work should explore extended feature sets, enhanced geometric descriptors, and expanded object categories to broaden the operational utility of the system. Because GB-CACHE maintains deterministic execution time, these additions can be integrated while preserving its suitability for real-time operation.

Advances in calibration also offer opportunities for improvement. The spatial calibration process, while highly accurate, required manual refinement when aligning LiDAR and camera measurements to shared features in the environment. Much of this process could be automated through a ROS-based calibration tool that identifies common geometric structures and iteratively adjusts the extrinsic parameters. Such a tool would reduce human involvement, improve repeatability, and accelerate recalibration when sensors are replaced or repositioned. Automation in this area would significantly enhance the practicality of deploying multi-modal systems in the field.

Temporal calibration likewise presents an important direction for refinement. The timing architecture developed in this dissertation relies on a GPS-disciplined clock distributed through NTP and refined through PTP, followed by embedding synchronized timestamps into the video stream through Supplemental Enhancement Information. Although this system performed reliably, it still contains two independent timestamp pathways that may introduce subtle delays under heavy load or complex networking conditions. Future work should consolidate these pathways into a single authoritative timestamp source and characterize any software-induced delays within the camera pipeline and streaming system. These improvements would further reduce timing uncertainty and support more advanced fusion strategies.

A final recommendation is the expansion of data collection across a broader range of environmental conditions. Maritime perception systems must operate under diverse lighting, weather, and sea-state scenarios. Expanding the dataset to capture greater variability will improve the generalization of both neural and deterministic methods and support learning-based approaches that require exposure to a wide range of conditions. Environmental diversity will also strengthen the evaluation of multi-modal fusion under challenging scenarios and increase the applicability of the results to real-world maritime operations.
